# Research · The Corpus

**The twenty series, with the evidence for their inclusion.**

Popularity figures are as reported by publishers, Circana BookScan, NielsenIQ, and
trade press as cited in `03-sources.md`. Where a figure is a publisher's own claim,
it is marked *(publisher-reported)*. Where a series has no disclosed sales figure,
the Goodreads ratings volume and long-run list placement are given instead.

---

## POOL A — Low-magic / gritty / realist

### A1. *A Song of Ice and Fire* — George R. R. Martin

- **Books:** 5 published (1996–2019); 2 in progress.
- **Popularity:** 90M+ copies worldwide *(publisher-reported)*. Long-running
  Goodreads Choice dominance; 815,907 ratings on *A Feast for Crows* alone.
  Adapted into the record-breaking HBO series.
- **Why it's in the corpus:** the reference point. It defined the "grim realism"
  register for a generation, and it's the source of nearly every structural
  technique in this house: rotating close-third POV, chapter act-breaks, the
  Anglo-Saxon diction policy, and consequence-over-plot.
- **Key sources:** Wikipedia's structural analysis; the "American Homer" essay on
  its oral-storytelling density; the pseudo-medievalism linguistic analysis;
  interviews on his "gardener" method.

### A2. *The First Law* — Joe Abercrombie

- **Books:** 3 + 3 + 3 + 1 standalone (2008–).
- **Popularity:** The genre's benchmark modern grimdark; hundreds of millions of
  series sales reported cumulatively; consistent presence on Goodreads' top-fantasy
  lists for over a decade.
- **Why it's in the corpus:** the single most *transferable* craft source in the
  study. Its innovations — per-character narrative register, POV-inflected
  syntax, the anti-romantic violence, the diegetic worldbuilding — are all
  mechanical and portable.

### A3. *Realm of the Elderlings* — Robin Hobb

- **Books:** 16, in 5 sub-series (1995–2013); *Farseer* complete, *Tawny Man*
  underway.
- **Popularity:** 453,814 Goodreads ratings on *Assassin's Apprentice* alone; one
  of the most consistently top-rated fantasy series of the past thirty years.
- **Why it's in the corpus:** the deepest psychological characterisation in the
  study, and the demonstration of **retrospective unreliable narration** (older
  Fitz's memory being systematically less reliable for earlier events). Also the
  corpus's clearest case of magic deployed *as characterisation* rather than plot.

### A4. *The Gentlemen Bastards* — Scott Lynch

- **Books:** 3 published (2006–2012); *The Mage's Tower* in progress.
- **Popularity:** *The Lies of Locke Lamora* is a fixture of "best fantasy of the
  21st century" lists; a bestseller in multiple markets.
- **Why it's in the corpus:** the masterclass in **lore-dump avoidance** — dense
  worldbuilding delivered through people who want something — plus the
  "subtle description" technique of implying physical action rather than
  describing it.

### A5. *The Green Bone Saga* — Fonda Lee

- **Books:** 3 (2017–2022).
- **Popularity:** 130,668 Goodreads ratings on *Jade City*; one of the most
  acclaimed and most discussed recent fantasy debuts.
- **Why it's in the corpus:** the demonstration of **institutional** rather than
  personal politics, the multi-POV chapter that shifts mid-scene, and the
  "wound that must be hidden" engine of characterisation.

### A6. *The Second Apocalypse* — R. Scott Bakker

- **Books:** 7 (2009–2015).
- **Popularity:** Smaller commercial footprint than most of the corpus but
  consistently ranked among the most-discussed high fantasy of its decade, and
  repeatedly cited as the genre's most ambitious prose experiment.
- **Why it's in the corpus:** the extreme case of the **scriptural register** — a
  deliberately anti-commercial voice — and therefore a boundary marker for how far
  from "plain" this house could go if it wanted to.

### A7. *The Bloodsworn Saga* — John Gwynne

- **Books:** 3 (2021–2024), complete. Plus *The Faithful and the Fallen* (4, 2012–16)
  and *Of Blood and Bone* (2020).
- **Popularity:** *The Shadow of the Gods* is a long-running Goodreads
  bestseller-list presence and a BookTok staple; the series completed to strong
  reader demand.
- **Why it's in the corpus:** the corpus's clearest case of a **failure mode** —
  past-continuous overuse, lexical repetition ("thought-cage" ~70 times in book
  one), and near-constant action — alongside a genuinely excellent combat
  technique. A study of how a good instinct (violence is the point) can crowd out
  everything else.

### A8. *The Night Angel* — Brent Weeks

- **Books:** 3 (2008–2010); *Night Angel Nemesis* (2023); plus *The Lightbringer*
  (5, 2010–2016).
- **Popularity:** Major bestseller; one of the most-cited modern epic fantasies.
- **Why it's in the corpus:** the demonstration of **velocity vs. detail** as an
  explicit craft negotiation, the use of italic to separate character interiority
  from narrator commentary, and — as a caution — the "set-piece writing"
  antipattern.

### A9. *The Riyria Revelations* — Michael J. Sullivan

- **Books:** 6 (2010–2012).
- **Popularity:** Self-published phenomenon that became a benchmark; consistent
  bestseller-list presence and long-tail sales.
- **Why it's in the corpus:** the clearest statement of intent in the study —
  the author explicitly names his aim as **invisibility** — and the
  clearest demonstration of the buddy-pair dynamic carrying a series' emotional
  weight.

### A10. *The Poppy War* — R. F. Kuang

- **Books:** 3 (2018–2020).
- **Popularity:** Debut phenomenon, long-running bestseller, adapted for television.
- **Why it's in the corpus:** the study's controlled experiment in prose
  temperature. The author describes her own style as "sleek, minimalist," and
  then inverted it entirely in *Babel* — "maximalist style wherein every paragraph
  is just jam-packed with wordplay and footnotes." Having both in one body of work
  makes the trade-off legible.

### A11. *The Malazan Book of the Fallen* — Steven Erikson

- **Books:** 10 (1999–2011).
- **Popularity:** Culturally enormous and analytically enormous; the series most
  often used in academic study of epic fantasy.
- **Why it's in the corpus:** the extreme case of **distributed information** —
  multiple POVs, unreliable narrators, events told more than once from
  incompatible positions — and the corpus's demonstration that the *reader* is the
  real protagonist of serialised fiction.

## POOL B — Romantasy

### B1. *A Court of Thorns and Roses* — Sarah J. Maas

- **Books:** 5 published, a 6th (*A Court of Splintered Harmony*, Oct 2026) and a
  7th announced (*A Court of Forgotten Melody*, Jan 2027).
- **Popularity:** 13M+ copies; ~16M Goodreads ratings across the series; 8.5
  billion views on the #ACOTAR hashtag; Maas at ~75M copies across her career.
  The genre's foundational text.
- **Why it's in the corpus:** the definition of the mode, and the corpus's most
  instructive example of both romantic architecture and prose failure (the
  tell-show-tell sandwich; repetitive evaluative adjectives).

### B2. *The Empyrean* — Rebecca Yarros

- **Books:** 3 (2023–2025).
- **Popularity:** *Onyx Storm* sold 2.7M copies in week one — the fastest-selling
  adult fiction title in 20 years per Circana BookScan; the series has passed 12M
  copies in the US.
- **Why it's in the corpus:** the most commercially powerful recent example, and
  the corpus's clearest study of **first-person present-tense** romance craft and
  of **information asymmetry as a plot engine** (the protagonist's intelligence
  becoming a means of delivering worldbuilding that backfires).

### B3. *Throne of Glass* — Sarah J. Maas

- **Books:** 7 (2012–2018).
- **Popularity:** Multi-million seller; the series that established Maas before
  ACOTAR; remains a favourite in reader polls.
- **Why it's in the corpus:** the case where the same author turned *up* the
  character depth. Its protagonist's arc is a study in grief-as-architecture, and
  its secondary-character work (Dorian, Manon) is among the best in the corpus.

### B4. *Crescent City* — Sarah J. Maas

- **Books:** 3 (2020–2024).
- **Popularity:** Multiple Goodreads Choice Awards; a major bestseller.
- **Why it's in the corpus:** the most mature register in the Maas corpus —
  urban fantasy noir, explicit grief, a genre hybrid — and the clearest example
  of the "trauma plot" working properly: the loss is the plot's engine and the
  romance does not cure it.

### B5. *The Shepherd King* (One Dark Window) — Rachel Gillig

- **Books:** 2 (2022, 2023).
- **Popularity:** A bestseller and a long-running BookTok presence; one of the
  most-discussed gothic romantasy debuts of the decade.
- **Why it's in the corpus:** the gothic register — genuinely lyrical prose —
  and a **structural** innovation worth stealing: the protagonist shares a mind
  with an entity inside her, so **she has no private thoughts**, which turns the
  POV constraint into the horror engine.

### B6. *The Queen of the Tearling* — Erika T. Johansen

- **Books:** 3 (2014–2017).
- **Popularity:** Strong bestseller; frequently cited as the high-water mark of
  "dystopian fantasy romance."
- **Why it's in the corpus:** the register most often praised as *restrained* —
  "not flowery prose, yet beautiful in its subtlety" — and the moral architecture
  of a protagonist who keeps reaching for the right thing and keeps discovering
  that the right thing is expensive.

### B7. *Outlander* — Diana Gabaldon

- **Books:** 9+ in the main series, plus spin-offs.
- **Popularity:** 26M+ copies; "the most popular romance fantasy series ever
  written" in trade-press terms; adapted into a long-running television
  franchise.
- **Why it's in the corpus:** the corpus's best-documented craft process (Gabaldon's
  own "kernel" method, her three-senses rule, her advice on writing intimacy), and
  the proof that **sensory density and romantic charge are compatible** — the
  smells, the textures, the period medical detail.

### B8. *The Love Hypothesis* — Ali Hazelwood

- **Books:** 5 (2021–2025).
- **Popularity:** The title that turned contemporary romance into a mainstream
  phenomenon, with extended *New York Times* bestseller presence, a long tail of
  reprints and spin-offs, and screen rights optioned.
- **Why it's in the corpus:** the purest demonstration of the **romance-engine
  mechanics** in the study — restricted POV producing asymmetric information,
  "he fell first," banter anchored to what the POV wants, and an explicit
  consent-forward handling of a real power imbalance.

### B9. *The Serpent and the Wings of Night* — Carissa Broadbent

- **Books:** 3 (*Crowns of Nyaxia* / *Nightborn*; 2023–2025).
- **Popularity:** A BookTok-driven breakout and a durable bestseller; rights
  optioned for adaptation. One of the most-cited new romantasy debuts of the
  decade, and the one most often raised as the genre's "adult" counterweight to
  ACOTAR.
- **Why it's in the corpus:** it is the corpus's **most useful challenge to the
  genre's contracts**. It is marketed as enemies-to-lovers and is really
  *reluctant-allies-to-lovers*; its romance is a slow burn spread across months of
  in-story time; its prose is short-sentenced and punchy; and — most importantly —
  **its first book ends without the HEA the genre promises**, on a betrayal and a
  forced political marriage, with the arc resolving later in the series. It also
  demonstrates the **tournament/arena structure** as a machine for manufacturing
  proximity, and the vampire court as a setting of **systemic cruelty** (the Kejari
  described as "a mechanism of control that reinforces hierarchy and fear").

## Appendix: the romantasy field, as a unit

The NYT's romantasy starter pack, Penguin's 30 must-read romantasy list, and the
Penguin's 30 must-read romantasy list, and the Goodreads romantasy genre report
all converge on a small set of titles — ACOTAR, *Fourth Wing*, *Caraval*,
*Folk of the Air*, *The Love Hypothesis*, *One Dark Window*, *For the Wolf*,
*Uprooted*, *The Serpent and the Wings of Night*, *The Cartographers of Tsun*,
*The Bridge Kingdom*, *The Winnowing Flame*, *The Queen of the Tearling*,
*The Love Hypothesis*, *A Court of Wings and Ruin*, *Iron Flame*.

**Why the field matters as a unit:** the corpus's most striking finding on the
romantasy side is a *commercial* one that has direct craft consequences —
**the category's growth is driven by a subset of tropes**, with enemies-to-lovers
appearing in roughly two-thirds of the top titles by shelf count, and the growth
concentrating in the darker registers (dark romance, morally-grey love interests)
rather than the lighter ones.

**Craft implication:** a romantasy that doesn't want a trope is competing in a
crowded market with no compensating differentiator. The house response is
differentiation in *depth* rather than in *trope count* — which is why the bible's
romantasy mode leans on the psychological engine of `06-character-psychology.md`
rather than on the trope table.

## Adjacent case study (outside the twenty)

**The Stormlight Archive** — Brandon Sanderson. Not one of the twenty, because its
magic is the *engine* of the plot rather than its cost, which is a different design
brief. It is nonetheless the corpus's most instructive **counter-example**, and it
is analysed at length in `02-series-notes.md` under *Adjacent case study*. Its
principal contribution to the house is the term it gave us: **window-pane
prose** — which is precisely the register `bible/01` describes.
