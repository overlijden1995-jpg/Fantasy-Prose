# Research · Series Notes

**Per-series craft findings.**

Each entry follows the four questions from `00-method.md`:

1. What does the prose do that the story doesn't need it to do?
2. What does the prose refuse to do?
3. How is a character built from a wound, a want and a contradiction?
4. What is the most transferable mechanism, and where does it break?

Every entry ends with **Adopted** and **Not adopted** — the second list is as
important as the first.

---

# POOL A — Low-magic / gritty / realist

## A1 · *A Song of Ice and Fire* — George R. R. Martin

### 1. Prose contribution
Martin establishes the register's *baseline*: fast, plain, functional, with an
unusually disciplined **low-density sentence**. An essay on the series as oral
storytelling notes that "except in cases of emotional shock, you never have to read
a *AGOT* sentence twice," and attributes this to a need for flow — the reader
must be able to consume a sentence at a glance because oral storytelling doesn't
allow backtracking.

The **diction policy** is the masterwork. Linguistic analysis documents a
*systematic* exclusion of Latinate, Greek, and neo-classical vocabulary in favour
of Anglo-Saxon and Norse roots. The effect is cumulative: the world sounds old
because its words are old. The same analysis identifies the pseudo-medieval layer
(*oft*, *elsewhere*, *amongst*, dropped *-ly* adverbs, invented compounds) as a
**blemish** on an otherwise clear style — the depth comes from the Germanic
vocabulary, not from the archaisms.

### 2. What the prose refuses to do
- No lyricism in the body of the text.
- No consolation. Deaths are not mourned into significance; they are *absences*.
- No plot armour for anyone, including POV characters.
- No explanation of the world's rules beyond what the characters need.

### 3. Character psychology
Martin's characters are built from **position**: a person's place in a hierarchy
determines their options, their self-image, and their relationship to everyone
else. Tyrion's wit is not a personality trait; it is a *compensation structure*
built over a lifetime of being the small person in the room. The prose renders it
as the reflexive joke that arrives before the thought.

The psychology is also rendered through **the gap between what a character believes
about themselves and what the reader can see** — the book is full of people who are
wrong about themselves and wrong about each other, and the prose never corrects
them.

### 4. Most transferable mechanism
**Chapter act-breaks.** Martin's screenwriting background taught him to end each
chapter "on a tense or revelational moment, a twist or a cliffhanger." This
combined with the "cut out the fat and leave the muscle" editing discipline
produced the series' defining rhythm.

**Where it breaks:** Martin writes chapters that end on turns *of the same kind*
often enough that the technique becomes legible. Variety in the kind of ending is
required for the technique to keep its force.

**A second mechanism:** the *responsiveness of worldbuilding to story*. Martin
describes beginning *A Game of Thrones* with a chapter and only afterwards asking
what kind of world could have produced it — the map drawn on typing paper with the
paper size partly determining the continent, as a *contradiction-prevention
system* rather than an aesthetic exercise.

### Adopted
- The diction policy (`bible/01`, `03`).
- Chapter act-breaks with **varied turn types** (`bible/04`).
- "Character is built from position" as a psychology engine (`bible/06`).
- Consequence over prophecy (`bible/04`, Law VI).

### Not adopted
- The pseudo-medievalism layer. One per page maximum.
- The habit of ending every chapter on a cliffhanger.
- The information density of a Martin's late chapters — the study notes the
  "information density" quality as a *choice*, not a default.

## A2 · *The First Law* — Joe Abercrombie

### 1. Prose contribution
Abercrombie's stated method, in his own words: "to give the different point of view
characters their own tone of voice, to let the feel of the prose hopefully
communicate something about that character." He describes it as "an extremely close
third person which uses changes in writing style to reflect the inner voice of the
characters."

The documented specifics are extraordinary and highly portable:

- One POV character's thoughts are rendered **entirely in italics**, continuously,
  which makes his internal monologue formally distinct from everyone else's.
- Another (the northern captain) speaks in an **earthy dialect** and the narration
  follows him into it.
- Another (the swordswoman) has **short, staccato sentences, long paragraphs, and
  emphasised single lines** — and, because she is colour-blind, **her descriptive
  passages contain no colour words at all**, which flattens her emotional register
  as a side effect.

Abercrombie describes the revision method: going over all the chapters from a given
POV together for days, "try and get as much of the feel of that character's
individual voice as possible into my head and onto the page."

### 2. What the prose refuses to do
Abercrombie's stated refusals, from interviews:
- **No beautiful violence.** "The violence is fumbly and messy and not beautiful or
  skilful; it's horrible, instantaneous and unpleasant."
- **No glamour sex.** He wanted the sex "to feel on a par with the rest" — honest
  and earthy.
- **No moral verdicts.** The reader is never told who is right.
- No lyrical register for any POV, including the aristocratic one.

### 3. Character psychology
The corpus's clearest study of **sympathy for the unlikeable**, and the mechanism
is precise:

1. **Real competence.** The torturer is genuinely excellent at his job, and the
   prose renders his expertise with the same precision it would render a surgeon's.
2. **A body that costs.** Pain is present in every scene — stairs, the ache in his
   back, how far he can walk. The disability is structural, not sentimental.
3. **A reason the reader can accept if not forgive.**
4. **One thing they do right, consistently, at cost.**

The second and third POVs (the noble, the barbarian) are built on **the gap between
what they say and what they mean**: the noble says what he wants to say before he
says what he means, and the barbarian's catchphrases are a *defence mechanism*
deployed against a life he finds unbearable.

### 4. Most transferable mechanism
**Per-character narrative register.** You can identify the POV from the first
paragraph with no identifying information. This is the single most transferable
technique in the study, and it costs nothing at the sentence level — it is a matter
of deciding, per character, what their attention is and what their language is.

**Where it breaks:** the same craft carries a risk the reception notes point out
repeatedly — **catchphrase overuse**. The corpus notes it as "a style in which
the prose is not as polished as Abercrombie's, and 'one of the things that most
often detractors mention is the over-reliance on the repetitious use of character
catchphrases.'" The technique is right; the volume is the problem.

### Adopted
- Per-character narrative register, including **perceptual limitation** as a
  filter (`bible/05`, `samples/01`).
- The italicised interior monologue as a formal distinction (`bible/02` §5).
- The four-step method for sympathy with the unlikeable (`bible/06` §4).
- Fumbly, messy, non-beautiful violence (`bible/09`).
- No moral verdicts in the prose (`bible/05` Rule 5).

### Not adopted
- The continuous italic monologue as a *whole-book* convention. Effective once per
  character, but the study notes it as an "almost exclusively for this character"
  technique — the scarcity is what makes it work.
- Catchphrase density. The mechanism, yes; the repetition, no.

## A3 · *Realm of the Elderlings* — Robin Hobb

### 1. Prose contribution
Hobb's prose is described by *The Times* as having "a sinewy simplicity close to
that of myths and fairytales." The corpus's own observation is the sharpest: she
is **not** using particularly complex words or sentence structures — "it's more
about having a strong talent for selecting the *correct* words and *correct*
sentence structures to make it work."

This is the counterweight to the "beautiful prose" idea, and it's a central
finding of the study: **the strongest prose in the corpus is not the most
ornamented.**

Hobb also **varies the prose style with the age of the narrator** in the *Farseer*
books — the childhood Fitz's thoughts feel disconnected, the adult Fitz's coherent.
This is a technique for rendering cognitive development *through syntax*.

### 2. What the prose refuses to do
- No epic battle sequences. No armies marching. Conflicts are intrapersonal.
- No magic-as-combat. The Skill and the Wit are tools for characterisation.
- No easy resolution, and no feel-good endings.
- No consolation for trauma.

### 3. Character psychology
Hobb's own statement of method: "Every action her characters take is based on
deeper reasoning and motivation... What shaped that character? Why is she so
suspicious of someone being kind to her, why does he think it's okay to be abusive
to someone?"

**The core psychological device is the character whose trauma has become a
generalised distrust of kindness.** This is the *Lie* in the house's six-part
engine, and Hobb renders it as behaviour: the flinch at being helped, the
refusal of comfort, the test she sets up to confirm that people will leave.

**The retrospective unreliability** is the corpus's most elegant psychological
device: an older Fitz narrating a younger Fitz's life, where **the further back
the event, the less reliable the telling** — because the older Fitz has since
lived through things that reframe the earlier material. The reader learns late
that some of what they were told was wrong.

The critical reception also surfaces a **counter-pattern worth naming**: the study
of Hobb's women found a consistent habit of using sexual violence as a
characterisation shortcut and of defining female characters through motherhood.
A bible that copies "trauma as personality" without checking whether the trauma
*does* anything would reproduce this.

### 4. Most transferable mechanism
**Memory as a live character.** The past isn't backstory; it's a filter that
changes what the character notices, believes, and does on the current page. It
fires at predictable stimuli.

**Where it breaks:** if the trauma only ever *describes* the character rather than
*governing* them, it becomes a label. The reception findings are the warning.

### Adopted
- The six-part psychology engine, with the Lie as the load-bearing element
  (`bible/06`).
- Retrospective unreliability as a technique (`bible/05` Rule 4).
- Magic as characterisation rather than plot (`bible/08` §5).
- Prose age-variation by narrator stage (noted as a technique for cognitive
  development).
- The "trauma must do something" rule, and its counter-patterns (`bible/06` §5).

### Not adopted
- The full "sinewy simplicity" register — Hobb's is a specific and beautiful
  thing, not a template. The house takes the *principle* (simple words, correctly
  chosen) and not the manner.
- The prevalence of animal-idiom characterisation. Powerful, but it becomes a
  tic if applied broadly.

## A4 · *The Gentlemen Bastards* — Scott Lynch

### 1. Prose contribution
Lynch writes **dense, highly detailed prose** and yet the reception consistently
reports that the detail never becomes a drag — the technique is the *selection* of
detail, described in a craft analysis as "the art of subtle description":

> "There's no description of action, but it's easy to picture his hand twitching,
> then his eyes turning to the place where the digit used to be. If we missed the
> mention of nine fingers before... now we know for certain it's missing."

Lynch **implies action rather than describing it**, using dialogue and reaction to
carry the physical information. In the same analysis, when describing a
neighbourhood, "he doesn't give us any description of the buildings, or the
streets, or the docks... Instead, Lynch focuses on the sailors — not what they look
like, but what happens to them."

### 2. What the prose refuses to do
- No info-dump, despite the density of the world. The reception credits him with
  "weaving lore deftly through the prose and plot to the point that world-building
  exposition doesn't make you pause."
- No glamour for the con. The heist is work.

### 3. Character psychology
Locke is built on a **contradiction**: he's a thief who operates by a moral code
(only the nobility, never the poor) which he maintains at personal cost and which
he cannot explain to anyone. He is also *explicitly* a "bad conman" in his own
narration — and the reception notes the craft achievement of keeping his dialect
consistent while letting his *persona* change with the mask.

The crew is built as a **found family whose loyalty is expressed through the
texture of how they argue.** Nobody declares friendship; the reader infers it from
the way they insult each other.

### 4. Most transferable mechanism
**Lore delivery through people who want something.** Lynch's world is enormous and
the reader never feels lectured, because every piece of it arrives attached to a
transaction, an insult, or a negotiation.

**Where it breaks:** the reception also notes the near-obsessive use of colourful
profanity, and one reviewer calls it "almost obsessive use of swear words... the
frequency with which it was used seemed to mirror that of a teenager attempting to
make himself look cool." The mechanism is sound; the *volume* is a choice with a
cost.

### Adopted
- Subtle description: imply physical action via reaction and dialogue.
- Detail selection over detail volume — describe *what happens to* the sailors, not
  the streets.
- Lore through wanters (`bible/08`).
- Found family expressed through the texture of argument (`bible/07`).

### Not adopted
- Profanity density. Register is a house decision and ours is drier.
- The full Victorian-Camorr manner. The *principle* (a specific city with its own
  smells, sounds, and social structure) is portable; the manner isn't.

## A5 · *The Green Bone Saga* — Fonda Lee

### 1. Prose contribution
Lee's prose is characterised as "relatively straightforward and clean, not
flowery, allowing you to dig deep into the story." Her combat sequences are
described as "glorious, cinematic" and "well oriented," the latter being
significant — the study of the series repeatedly praises the *clarity* of the
fighting.

**The structural device worth stealing: the chapter that shifts POV mid-scene**,
which "helps make this feel like a novelized movie."

### 2. What the prose refuses to do
Lee refuses to let the institution be decorative. The Green Bone clans are
*legal institutions* with a history, and the politics are **institutional and
factional** rather than a web of individuals scheming against each other — the
series is compared to a Cold War epic more than a dynastic one.

### 3. Character psychology
The engine is the **hidden wound**. *Strange Horizons*' review is the precise
analysis:

> "Injuries that must be hidden and stories that must not be told accumulate
> heavily on particular characters... the beautiful, violent, balletic sequencing
> of a fight to the death between Green Bone warriors, and the slow,
> behind-the-scenes erosion of jade-burdened, honor-conscious, unconfessed human
> psyches... intertwined."

And on Hilo specifically: "Hilo's deliberately selective account of his cousin's
heredity is an attempt to erase and ease the cousin's indelible doubts and hurts."

**The contradiction:** a character who feels everything at full volume and expresses
all of it — which makes them honest and loyal and *also* controlling and sometimes
cruel. "It makes him honest and loyal, but also controlling and sometimes cruel."

### 4. Most transferable mechanism
**The wound that must not be spoken.** A character's most important experience is
one they cannot admit, and the prose renders the *suppression* — the evasive
account, the deflection, the story told to Anden about his mother. This is the
house's "the Lie" operating at the level of *narration*, and it's very strong.

**Where it breaks:** the reception of the second volume notes the opposite failure
— over-explanation. *"She is so focused on explaining every small detail that every
other aspect of the book is obscured by this mass of information"* and a summary
of conversations and statements of feeling rather than dramatisation. This is the
info-dump antipattern (D1) and the show-don't-tell antipattern (C7) in combination.

### Adopted
- The hidden wound (`bible/06` §3.5, Law VIII).
- Institutional politics as a frame (`bible/06` §7).
- Mid-scene POV shift, used once and earned (`bible/05`).
- Clarity in combat (`bible/09`).

### Not adopted
- The mythology interludes as a routine structural device. Effective as an
  occasional break; a default would cost us the ground-level grit that the series
  does better than almost anything in the corpus.

## A6 · *The Second Apocalypse* — R. Scott Bakker

### 1. Prose contribution
Bakker writes in "a sort of epic poem/scriptural style, which feels like a
combination of the bible and a translation of the Iliad," and the study notes
that at times his prose "channels the brutality of McCarthy, at others it's more
like reading the Bible." His own influence set — *Dune*, Tolkien, the First
Crusade — is documented.

The prose is **dense with philosophy and introspection**, and the corpus's readers
describe it as "information dense" rather than "purple dense" — the text implies
more than it says.

### 2. What the prose refuses to do
- The reader is never handed the meaning. The study notes that "he doesn't spell
> out the meanings," and that "the text always implies more than what is written,
> again relying on the reader to think and fill in the blanks themselves."
- He starts mid-plot. *Gardens of the Moon* opens inside a conflict with no
orientation.
- He refuses to resolve the series' central theological question.

### 3. Character psychology
Bakker's characters are **distorted archetypes** — and the study's examples are
the clearest statement in the corpus of what "deconstructing the trope" actually
means: "What if Conan the Barbarian was a deeply closeted gay man, his mind
cracking under the strain? What if Aragorn was a ruthless sociopath?"

The series' thematic engine is **doubt** — "the entire series is about doubt... How
far one can be pushed in either direction before the breaking point." Bakker uses
an "exceptionally brutal world" as an instrument to *torture* the characters and
examine how they respond.

### 4. Most transferable mechanism
**Brutality as a character-revealing instrument rather than as spectacle.** The
world is harsh *on purpose*: it is the test through which character is observed.
This is the strongest argument in the corpus against the idea that grit is a tone.

**Where it breaks:** the accessibility cost. The study repeatedly reports the
prose as "dense, philosophical writing, overly descriptive prose, and extremely
dark and mature themes — which have prevented its work from receiving much
mainstream attention." And the *First Law* comparison in the Reddit thread
("little cartoony… less artful… it can feel like more 'super powers fantasy'") is
the general reader's verdict on over-saturation.

### Adopted
- Brutality as a test that reveals character (`bible/09`).
- **The reader's work as a form of respect** — the implication-over-statement
  principle (`bible/06` §3).
- Refusal to resolve the thematic question on the page.

### Not adopted
- The scriptural register. It is spectacular and it is a **boundary marker**, not a
  target. The bible's dial includes position 8 for a handful of lines per book.
- Beginning mid-plot with no orientation. `bible/04` §6 keeps the information
  withheld while holding the orientation — the study's own finding is that this is
  where *Malazan* loses readers.
- The philosophical density in interior monologue. We take the *implication*; we
  don't take the treatise.

## A7 · *The Bloodsworn Saga* — John Gwynne

### 1. Prose contribution
Gwynne's combat is the best in the corpus for *clarity*, and it comes from
specific training — he researched Viking combat and the specificity is visible.
His background in professional sport brings "physical specificity to combat
writing that distinguishes his battle scenes from the field."

### 2. What the prose refuses to do
He refuses to let the war be clean. The corpus's summary: "Gwynne never
glorifies war. While the tactical action is incredibly thrilling, his prose
consistently highlights the profound grief, physical trauma, and collateral ruin
that violence leaves behind on communities."

He also grounds the emotional register in the domestic before the epic: *The Shadow
of the Gods* opens with "several chapters of Orka's domestic life before
destroying it — the specificity of what she loses is what makes the violence that
follows land differently from generic revenge plots."

### 3. Character psychology
**Found family over blood.** Every one of his series is built on people who choose
each other through shared survival. The emotional core is the warband, not the
romance and not the political intrigue.

The **technique worth taking** is the domestic-before-the-catastrophe structure:
establish what a character has, in a quiet scene, so that the destruction of it
means something. This is the same principle as the hidden wound, applied to
*place* rather than to *self*.

### 4. Most transferable mechanism
**Establish the ordinary before you destroy it.** A chapter of quiet, specific
domestic life is the setup for the catastrophe, and it costs a page.

**Where it breaks — the corpus's clearest failure mode:** repetition. The
reception is unusually specific about it:
- The word "thought-cage" (meaning *mind*) appears ~70 times in book one, "as many
  as 5 per chapter."
- "The same description for doing something every single time" — shield wall
  formation, helmets, "long strides that ate up the ground."
- Past continuous used where past simple was required: "he might say 'she was
> turning' instead of 'she turned.' The former would only make sense if something
> else happened while the character was in the process of turning. But no."
- The near-constant action: "every single fucking chapter has to have an action
> sequence" — leaving no room for the character development the series is
  otherwise good at.

The corpus's verdict is the sharpest formulation of a truth this bible repeats:
**the problem is never the prose's simplicity. The problem is the volume.**

### Adopted
- Establish the ordinary before destroying it.
- Combat clarity through specific study, not generalisation (`bible/09`).
- The civilian cost of war, on the page (`bible/09`).
- Found family as the emotional core.

### Not adopted
- **Repetition as texture.** The saga-effect of recurring phrases is a legitimate
  Norse-saga register and it is also the single biggest complaint about the
  series. House rule: a recurring phrase may appear **once per book**, and the
  word for *mind* may not become a coinage.
- **Past continuous as a cinematic default.** It reads as an error and it is.
  Present tense for immediacy; past simple for everything else.
- Action-per-chapter as a structural default.

## A8 · *The Night Angel* — Brent Weeks

### 1. Prose contribution
Weeks's prose is characterised as "a relentless, propulsive force, often employing a
clipped, impactful rhythm that plunges readers into a world thick with moral
ambiguity and imminent danger." And: "his prose rings like crystal; his dialogue
is witty, chatty, and brisk; and his pacing is frictionless."

His own craft statement is about the **velocity/detail negotiation**:

> "With a story that moves this fast, I think sometimes you get a view like you're
> looking out the window of a speeding train. You get a good sense of the landscape,
> but the details are lost. That's something I'm working on doing better — how to
> write prose with velocity that still keeps details sharp."

And on violence: "I don't always put the camera as it were right on the gore, but I
do always try to show that there are real consequences for the brutal things that
happen."

### 2. What the prose refuses to do
- No gratuitous shock. He is explicit: the books "manage this honesty, though,
> without being gratuitous or grimdark. There's no wallowing."
- No irony or false detachment: "There's no irony in these books, no pretend
  detachment. They're sincere, beginning to end."

### 3. Character psychology
Weeks's characters are **grotesques** — his own word — and he addresses the
caricature risk directly:

> "Now, the challenge when you use grotesques is that they can become caricatures.
> So the rest of your writing needs to be strong enough to differentiate these
> characters from each other so that they feel larger than life, rather than
> simply ludicrous."

His answer is bluntness: "these books are blunt and honest about the issues young
people face: big ugly issues like messy divorces and abandonment and heartbreak and
failure."

### 4. Most transferable mechanism
**Use italics to separate the character's interiority from the narrator's
commentary.** Weeks's craft analysis notes that italics distinguish "the parts of
the narrative coming from a narrator (not the character) from the character's own
time-bound, in-story, limited point of view."

**Where it breaks — the set-piece trap:** a well-argued thread in the corpus
identifies it precisely:

> "Some writers plan their book around dynamic scenes they envision, and attempt to
> transcribe the action directly from brain to page... it's only a sin when the
> immersion, reader enjoyment, or elements of the actual story are hurt bending
> to fit in what the writer deems just too cool to leave out."

And on the technique's symptom: "he tends to write them as being rather overly
complicated, including two or three important people, large piles of extras, and
(always) several sentences on the unique architecture required for the tool-cool
action scene to work."

Also noted: his POV rotation is so frequent that "each character, interesting as
they are, only provide the smallest of background" — the cost of the technique.

### Adopted
- Italic as the formal marker of character interiority (`bible/02` §5).
- Clipped rhythm under pressure (`bible/02` §2).
- Consequences over gore (`bible/09`).
- Sincere rather than ironic (`bible/01` §4).

### Not adopted
- The set-piece method, explicitly.
- Rapid POV rotation. Weeks rotates every few pages; the corpus's other masters
  (Martin, Abercrombie, Lee) rotate every chapter and hold. The house holds.

## A9 · *The Riyria Revelations* — Michael J. Sullivan

### 1. Prose contribution
Sullivan states the design goal explicitly and it is the most useful single
sentence in the study:

> "The style of prose I chose for *The Riyria Revelations* can best be described as
> straightforward and unadorned. My intention was to make the writing itself
> invisible to the reader and keep them focused on the characters and plot."

And his reasoning:

> "A complex plot would drown in a similar style... The *Riyria Revelations* was
> born out of my trying something new. Coming off a novel of deep prose, *Riyria*
> was a great leap to a story of simplicity... I attempted a style I had never
> tried before — **invisibility**."

He also documents the deliberate use of contractions in close third: "much of my
writing sounds like it came from the character. So if contractions are avoided,
the narrative can sound a bit formal and stilted."

### 2. What the prose refuses to do
- No ornament. No self-display.
- No lengthy reflection; the corpus notes he "reveals characterisation more
  through events than through lengthy reflection."
- No social commentary. The corpus specifically praises the absence of
  "social commentary or preaching."

### 3. Character psychology
Sullivan's engine is the **buddy pair**: a cynical thief and an idealist
mercenary, whose "disagreements create humor without making either man useless."
The formal device is that the *pair* is the character — each is individually
shallower than the sum, and the corpus notes this as both the technique's power
and its risk (the individual leads being "not as well-developed as one would
wish").

His second method: **the iceberg** — "I employ the iceberg approach to both
character and world building."

### 4. Most transferable mechanism
**Invisibility as a design target.** Not as a style, but as a *test*: does the
reader's attention stay on the story? Sullivan's related and highly portable
principle — a **simple plot with complex characters** versus a **complex plot
demanding simple style** — is a genuinely useful way to think about a whole book.

His related choice is worth noting because it saves thousands of words of
exposition: **he set the story in a medieval European world specifically so he
wouldn't have to explain things** — "I could trust that when I said the first born
son would be the next in line to be king rather than the last son or the daughter,
I didn't have to explain why."

### Adopted
- The invisibility test (`bible/01`).
- Contractions in close third (`bible/03`).
- Characterisation through events rather than reflection (`bible/06`).
- The buddy-pair structure as a series engine (`bible/10` §2, as a general
  relationship-carrying structure).
- Setting choice as an exposition-saving strategy (`bible/08`).

### Not adopted
- The "blank prose" reading of invisibility. The corpus is also clear that the
  style "has a very blank prose style which is effortless to read, but also
  somewhat forgettable." **Invisibility is not the absence of character in the
  language** — the language must still be *shaped by* the character. This is the
  exact point where Sullivan and Abercrombie differ, and the house takes
  Abercrombie's side.

## A10 · *The Poppy War* / *Babel* — R. F. Kuang

### 1. Prose contribution
This is the corpus's controlled experiment. Kuang's own account:

> "With the *Poppy War* trilogy, I was aiming for sleek, minimalist prose. With
> *Babel*, I'm exploring a maximalist style wherein every paragraph is just
> jam-packed with wordplay and footnotes and gossipy asides."

And on *Babel*'s ambition: "I think Kuang's writing style works because of the
ambition of her storytelling. If she wrote with more embellishments, flourishes,
varied and unpredictable sentence patterns, it would have rendered her more
complex sections inaccessible and self-indulgent. Kuang is self-indulgent, but never
at the expense of clarity."

The *Poppy War*'s prose is characterised as "urgent in the way it pulled the
reader along through the story. It wasn't necessarily beautiful, but it was
effective." *Babel*'s as "cleaner and more patient."

### 2. What the prose refuses to do
*Babel* refuses to trust the reader: "*Publishers Weekly* negatively reviewed the
novel, saying 'Kuang underwhelms with a didactic, unsubtle take on dark academia
and imperialism... the narrative frequently interrupted by lectures on why
imperialism is bad, not trusting the reader or the plot itself enough to know that
this message will be clear from the events as they unfold."

*Poppy War* refuses, at least in its characterisation, to let the reader forget
that the power is dangerous: "character psychology was the weakest aspect of *The
Poppy War*."

### 3. Character psychology
*Babel*'s beating heart is stated by the author as "the relationships between the
four members of the cohort — Robin, Ramy, Victoire, and Letty" — a friendship group
"where everyone is a little in love with one another, and where everyone hates one
another because of it."

That is the corpus's most precise formulation of a four-way romantic-dynamic
engine, and it is directly relevant to the house's romantasy mode.

### 4. Most transferable mechanism
**Prose temperature is a deliberate choice, and the choice is about what the book
needs.** Kuang did both minimalism and maximalism within one career and was
explicit that both were correct for their books.

**Where it breaks:** the lecture. *Babel* is the corpus's best-documented case of a
book whose prose worked and whose *structure* didn't — the theme was asserted
rather than dramatised, and the characters became mouthpieces.

### Adopted
- The explicit choice of prose temperature (`bible/01`, dial).
- The four-way friendship engine for romantasy (`bible/10`).
- Clarity preserved even in a maximalist register.

### Not adopted
- Footnotes carrying the story's argument. Notes as texture: yes. Notes as
  thesis: no.
- The lecture, in any form. `bible/11` E6.

## A11 · *The Malazan Book of the Fallen* — Steven Erikson

### 1. Prose contribution
The corpus's most extreme case of **information density**. Erikson "tells or shows
just enough of the world for the reader to picture the scene and give a little
context. The result you get is drip-fed information instead of big info dumps,
which has you constantly adjusting the picture in your head."

And: "the text always implies more than what is written, again relying on the
reader to think and fill in the blanks themselves."

His method of construction is documented: he wrote each chapter like a short
story, "Every word had to mean something, but he doesn't spell out the meanings."
And each book starts from "one cinematic scene."

### 2. What the prose refuses to do
- The reader is never given the whole picture. Erikson does not do exposition the
  way other fantasy does.
- He kills central characters.
- He will not resolve the series' central question (suffering) — and does not kill
  the antagonist.

### 3. Character psychology
**The soldier-focalisation.** The study notes that "the pages Erikson writes using
the voice of the soldier as a tool for philosophizing far outnumber any other
category of text one might think of," and that the soldier's viewpoint is
continuously varied in theme while maintaining the register. Erikson's stated
method: "I try to tackle specific themes in each novel, to give each work a
distinctive tone and flavour — it's not enough to hammer away on a single theme
(like 'war is a terrible thing and here's why') over and over again."

His thematic stance is the corpus's clearest statement of the moral position of
grimdark: Erikson described *Malazan* as a plea for compassion, and the study
notes that the books "do not shy away from the darker side of human nature" while
"juxtaposing this brutality with moments of startling compassion. These gestures
are often small, easily overlooked, but they provide emotional ballast against
despair."

### 4. Most transferable mechanism
**The same event told from multiple incompatible positions.** The corpus describes
"a grand unreliable semi-omniscient narrator and specific chapters and stories told
from very tightly focused unreliable narrators," and the effect: "we begin to
understand that nothing in this world (or ours for that matter) is necessarily
true simply because we read it."

**Where it breaks:** the accessibility cost. *Gardens of the Moon* drops the reader
into a conflict with no orientation. Erikson's own framing of the series as
inviting the reader in and "leaving them standing, looking round, wandering a bit
but not far" is generous; most readers experience it as a wall.

### Adopted
- The distributed-information architecture (`bible/05` §4).
- Drip-fed context over info-dumps (`bible/08`).
- The soldier register as a series-wide voice for a specific class of experience
  (`bible/06`).
- Small gestures of compassion as ballast against despair (`bible/09` §4).

### Not adopted
- Beginning mid-plot with no orientation. We withhold *why*, never *where*
  (`bible/04` §6).
- The ten-book density. The house will not sustain that much concurrency.
- The "nothing is necessarily true" licence applied to the reader's basic
  comprehension of the scene. A certain amount of footing is a kindness.

# POOL B — Romantasy

## B1 · *A Court of Thorns and Roses* — Sarah J. Maas

### 1. Prose contribution
Maas writes at a **high sensory temperature**: "her prose is lush, dramatic, and
emotionally heightened." The critical consensus is that the sensory abundance
serves the escapism goal — "it creates a sensory overload that makes the world feel
lush and oppressive simultaneously."

But the most useful finding is the **diagnostic** one: the study identifies the
prose's characteristic faults as melodrama, hyperbolic repetition, and stacked
evaluative adjectives — "Her characters are given Important Titles that truly have
no meaning behind them. Instead of showing us how mighty these characters are, we
are told of their grandness in larger-than-life terms. These titles add nothing to
the character themselves."

The other recurring criticism is the **tell-show-tell sandwich**: "She's the queen
of 'tell, then show, then let me tell you again!'" And the punctuation critique —
"over-uses asyndetons, em-dashes, participles, and, most grossly of all, absolute
phrases — which are essentially a sort of comma splice."

The *positive* finding: Maas is at her best when writing **relationships** —
"she's very good at writing deep, emotional connections between characters,
especially female characters" — and when handling **trauma as architecture**. The
study's description of the healing arc is the house's position restated: "She
doesn't suggest that a new romance 'fixes' the broken pieces. Instead, she
emphasizes the necessity of autonomy and the slow process of reclaiming one's
agency."

### 2. What the prose refuses to do
Refusing the *competence* of the reader. The book's central romantic revelation —
that the Beast's anger is abuse and that "true love" means consent — is structurally
delivered.

### 3. Character psychology
Trauma is the plot. The corpus's reading of the series is that Maas's innovation
was "to glue it all together with a trauma plot. Trauma and its integration into
the self are as fundamental to her books as, say, the need to stop an evil king
from destroying all humanity."

And the crucial limitation, which the house must inherit: in *ACOTAR* the trauma is
sometimes *described* rather than *behaved* — "heals" as a mood rather than a
process.

### 4. Most transferable mechanism
**The romance as a structural claim about power.** In the best of the series, the
moment the reader is told "it's your choice" is doing two things at once —
characterising the love interest and dismantling the previous love interest's
control. That is a *plot* beat disguised as a line of dialogue.

**Where it breaks:** the *Feyre* problem. The reception of the first books notes
"endless monologues" and "assumes Tamlin's thoughts and behavior," and the
characterisation criticism that "there's no psychological exploration... you've
got the angry one, the mean one, the sad one etc."

### Adopted
- The romance as a structural claim (`bible/10`).
- Trauma as architecture, with autonomy as the arc (`bible/06`).
- The relationship as the primary vehicle for character depth (`bible/10` §5.2).

### Not adopted
- Melodrama. Our register is the cool one.
- The tell-show-tell sandwich. Say it once.
- Repetitive evaluative adjectives (`bible/11` F4).
- Em-dash density: one per scene (`bible/02` §7).
- Titles as characterisation. We use position, not honorifics (`bible/03` §6).

## B2 · *The Empyrean* — Rebecca Yarros

### 1. Prose contribution
A scholarly review describes the technique precisely: Yarros "constructs this
premise through a **first-person, present-tense narration** that lends the prose an
immediacy verging on the cinematic."

The *achievement* the review identifies: "the treatment of embodiment and
disability. Violet's physical fragility, later specified as a connective-tissue
disorder, is not a decorative vulnerability to be narratively resolved but a
persistent condition she must continually negotiate."

### 2. What the prose refuses to do
Refuses to cure the protagonist's body. And refuses to explain the conspiracy
before it's actionable — the information arrives as *gaps* the protagonist notices.

### 3. Character psychology
**Competence developed against perceived inadequacy.** The reading notes that
"unlike many fantasy novels where the hero is immediately skilled or destined for
greatness, Violet Sorrengail begins as physically inadequate... This deliberate
setup of vulnerability is crucial."

And — the more interesting mechanism — **information asymmetry as plot engine**:
"Yarros limits Violet's access to information, rather than providing falsehoods,
mirroring real-world dynamics in authoritarian structures, where truth becomes a
scarce commodity." The protagonist's own intelligence is the vector for
worldbuilding, which is why the worldbuilding reads as *anxious* rather than
didactic.

### 4. Most transferable mechanism
**The protagonist's coping mechanism as the worldbuilding delivery system.** A
trained scribe reciting facts to manage her nerves is a *character* doing a
*character* thing, and the reader receives the lore without a lecture. This is a
genuinely elegant solution and it is directly transferable to the house's
`bible/08`.

**Where it breaks:** the same mechanism, over-used, becomes an info-dump. The
reception is unanimous: "Yarros is prone to info-dumping through Violet, who
often recites historical facts and texts in order to focus on a task or distract
herself from the grueling pain inflicted on her body... the reader is given
paragraphs of relatively sparse information without any time to digest the lore."

Also noted: the characters' voices converge — "All the characters talk the same
way. There's no effort made to make the characters talk differently based on
personality." And the anachronism problem: "she cannot avoid having her characters
speaking in 2020s idioms."

### Adopted
- The coping-mechanism worldbuilding vector (`bible/08` §2).
- First-person present tense as an option for the romantasy mode (`bible/10`).
- A body that is not cured (`bible/10` §5.1).
- Information asymmetry as plot engine (`bible/05` §4).

### Not adopted
- Anachronistic idiom. The house's register is period-neutral-modern at most.
- A single voice for the whole cast. The house requires six voice sheets before
  a chapter is written (`samples/01`).
- The book-length infobox: "the back third gets baggy and info-dumpy right when
  it should be tightening."

## B3 · *Throne of Glass* — Sarah J. Maas

### 1. Prose contribution
The relevant finding here is **tonal development**: *Throne of Glass* "begins as
YA-adjacent. Books 6–7 are not." The series deepens in darkness and speed, and
readers consistently identify *Crown of Midnight* as the point where "the series
becomes what it actually is."

The craft point for the house: **the same author, at higher volume, in a different
register** — and the corpus's readers note that the same source material's
characters become flat when the treatment is dumbed down: "Maas just rips off all
its characters, down to every last mannerism and turns of phrase, dumbs it down to
ya level, extracts any real depth or meaning."

### 2. What the prose refuses to do
*Throne of Glass* refuses the decorative. The series' most-admired feature is the
**grief-as-tattoo** device — Aelin inking her dead friends onto her body as a
literal ledger of loss. That is the corpus's most direct illustration of a
psychological state being rendered as a *physical practice* rather than as
description.

### 3. Character psychology
The secondary characters are the achievement. Dorian's arc is "the clearest study
of what a crown costs"; his chapters are darker than the surrounding story and his
recovery is "among the most honest depictions of trauma in the series." Manon's
chapters exist outside the protagonist's camp and have their own society and logic.

That last point is the house's principle: **a character not defined by proximity to
the protagonist.** It is how you avoid a cast of satellites.

### 4. Most transferable mechanism
**Grief as a physical practice.** When a character's loss needs to be on every page
without being discussed, give it a *procedure* — a tattoo, a ritual, a ledger, a
habit of counting. The practice does the emotional work permanently and silently.

### Adopted
- Grief as physical practice (`bible/06` §3).
- Characters with independent agendas not defined by proximity (`bible/06` §5).
- The tonal-development model: a series may move along the dial as it progresses
  (`bible/01` §2).

### Not adopted
- The first book's YA register. We don't downgrade; we write adult and let the
  reader come up.

## B4 · *Crescent City* — Sarah J. Maas

### 1. Prose contribution
The most adult register in the Maas corpus: "a painstakingly-crafted, cruel world
in defiance of the fairytal-ish-ness of *ACOTAR*," with "urban/high fantasy and
noir atmosphere of mystery, drugs, sex, war, and epicness all in one book."

### 2. What the prose refuses to do
Refuses the happy ending until it's earned *and* refuses to let the romance
resolve the grief.

### 3. Character psychology
**The loss is the plot.** The protagonist's best friend is murdered at the start
and the entire book is about a woman trying to survive the aftermath. And the
study's reading of the *structure* — that the first hundred pages are a slog
because the book description already told the reader the friend was going to die —
is a warning worth recording: **the reader shouldn't be able to see the death
coming.**

### 4. Most transferable mechanism
**The grief that isn't romanticised or cured.** The book is about the aftermath of
a death, and the death is not the plot's turn — it's the plot's *premise*.

### Adopted
- Grief as premise rather than plot (`bible/10` §5.1).
- The urban/noir register as a legitimate house position.

### Not adopted
- The 150-page ramp before the inciting incident.

## B5 · *The Shepherd King* (One Dark Window) — Rachel Gillig

### 1. Prose contribution
"Rachel Gillig's prose is something truly unique... It reads like poetry disguised
as narrative, dreamy and dark in equal measure. She reminded me of a gothic
Stephanie Garber, someone who tells stories like she's spinning a romantic
fairytale, but with teeth."

Her gothic apparatus is noted as deliberate and structural: rhyming incantations,
poetic epigraphs, the Red Riding Hood lineage, fog, woods, a corrupted court.

### 2. What the prose refuses to do
Refuses the standard POV privacy — and here's the innovation:

> "Elspeth's thoughts and stream-of-consciousness are 'shared' with Nightmare. Elspeth
> cannot have any private internal thoughts because Nightmare is in her mind,
> literally. At the same time, Nightmare is a sentient being who is so powerful
> that his thoughts, memories, and stream-of-consciousness bleed into Elspeth's
> mind."

### 3. Character psychology
The **cost-based magic** is the engine: every use of a Providence card extracts
something physical — and the corruption is visible in the body. The heroine's
monster is a *dependency*.

### 4. Most transferable mechanism
**Turn a POV constraint into the story's engine.** The shared consciousness does
three things at once: it creates the horror, it creates the romance's central
asymmetry (she cannot have a private reaction to him), and it creates the
formality of the prose. That is a mechanism, not a gimmick.

**Where it breaks:** the reception notes the YA-adjacent characterisation and the
overuse of em-dashes, and the "character development relies heavily on telling" —
"we are told repeatedly what a 'brutish' person Prince Hauth is."

### Adopted
- POV constraint as engine (`bible/05`).
- Cost-based magic with visible bodily consequence (`bible/08` §5).
- Gothic atmosphere as a legitimate register position (dial 4).

### Not adopted
- Rhyming incantations as a routine device. Once, and it must be good.
- The em-dash density.

## B6 · *The Queen of the Tearling* — Erika T. Johansen

### 1. Prose contribution
The most-quoted characterisation of the prose in the corpus, and it's a direct
statement of our house register:

> "Johansen's writing style is unique. It's not flowery prose, yet it is beautiful
> in its subtlety. Maybe it's because of the humanity contained in her words, even
> when she covers the hard, sad parts of the human experience."

The critical note: the magic "has been deftly muted in subordination to our
protagonists' evolution and growth."

### 2. What the prose refuses to do
Refuses the competent heroine. Kelsea is "neither physically attractive, nor
martially competent" — and the corpus notes this as the source of her
credibility: "she's so dedicated to her people that you can't help but cheer for
her."

It also refuses a clean moral frame. The story's moral question is genuinely
hard, and the protagonist reaches for the right thing repeatedly and discovers the
right thing is expensive.

### 3. Character psychology
**The protagonist who keeps choosing the expensive good.** The house's Law VI in
character form: she wants to do right, and doing right costs her, and she does it
again.

**The magic as borrowed, not possessed:** "The power is something that Kelsea
borrows rather than possesses, which is refreshing."

### 4. Most transferable mechanism
**A heroine who is right about values and incompetent at execution.** This creates
the arc without requiring the protagonist to be wrong. It also creates the
frustration the reader needs.

### Adopted
- Subtlety over flow (`bible/01`).
- The expensive-good protagonist (`bible/06`).
- Borrowed power (`bible/08` §5).

### Not adopted
- The frequent physical-appearance evaluation. The reception is right that it's
  exhausting and it undermines the protagonist.

## B7 · *Outlander* — Diana Gabaldon

### 1. Prose contribution
The best-documented craft process in the corpus. Gabaldon's method:

> "I start with something I can see or sense concretely — my 'kernel' — and I
> write that down, in a line or two that expresses what I see or hear or whatever,
> as concisely as possible."

And her sensory rule: "**If you use any three or more of the five senses in a
> scene, that scene will become three-dimensional**, and readers will feel like
> they're in there."

And her rhythm rule: "poetry is as much a matter of rhythm as of word-choice, and
the words in a passage form a pattern with the others."

Her published worked example is extraordinary and directly usable: she starts from
"the tiny heartbeat under my fingers was soft and stubborn," and then *reasons
her way out* to "the fleeting jolts life and the space between infinity" — and
then notices that the paragraph has taken "a similar rhythmic structure... first
sentence a two/three part simple, down-to-earth statement of physical
circumstance, followed by a longer, more complex and somewhat poetic sentence
dealing with the emotional."

That is a full craft lesson in a single worked example: **start from the
concrete sensation, and let the abstraction earn its way in.**

### 2. What the prose refuses to do
Refuses the "period-sandwiched" fantasy. She doesn't sand down 1743's violence or
medical reality. The study: "Gabaldon grounds the fantasy premise in
period-accurate medicine, letting Claire's modern nursing knowledge clash
productively with 18th-century treatment methods."

### 3. Character psychology
**Two vocabularies in one book.** The study notes that the dialogue "conveys the
cadences of 18th-century Scottish speech and vocabulary, peppered with occasional
Gaelic, in contrast to Claire's middle-class post-war English," and that
"misunderstandings arise not only because of her mid-20th century frame of
reference but her Englishness itself." The friction between the two voices *is* the
character relationship.

And the sex-scene craft, which the study isolates as the corpus's most transferable
romance lesson:

1. **Use dialogue to drive the scene.** The famous scene is mostly dialogue; the
   physical description is nearly absent and the reader understands exactly what
   happened.
2. **Keep it real.** "Jamie has sex like a virgin, and it's awesomely awkward at
   first."
3. **Build to a reveal or a reversal.** "In one way or another, a *reveal* or a
   *reversal* occurs" in the important scenes.

### 4. Most transferable mechanism
**Start from the kernel.** A concrete sensation, in one line, from which the whole
passage is developed. And: **three senses per scene**.

**Where it breaks:** the corpus's negative note is that Gabaldon's sensory
abundance sometimes buries the plot — "focusing on (largely irrelevant) details
while dismissing or missing the main thing." The same tendency appears in the
reception of the later books.

### Adopted
- The kernel method (`samples/00`).
- Three senses per scene (`bible/08` §6).
- The reveal/reversal structure for intimacy scenes (`bible/10` §3).
- Dialogue-driven intimacy over description (`bible/10`).

### Not adopted
- Sensory volume without a plot anchor. The house rule is three senses, not five.
- The romance-first structure *within* a very long serial. Our romantasy mode
  keeps the romance load-bearing without making it the only clock.

## B8 · *The Love Hypothesis* — Ali Hazelwood

### 1. Prose contribution
The clearest demonstration of the **romance-engine mechanics** in the corpus, and
the most transferable of the romantasy set for our purposes.

**The banter rule, stated by the study:** "as a writer I'm always watching how
romance authors keep banter from floating away from the plot — Hazelwood anchors
every joke to something Olive actually wants, which is why the funny scenes still
move the story."

**The specificity rule:** real scientific terminology, real grant politics, real
conference stress — "the academia texture (grant panic, conference politics, the
sacred lab schedule) gives the romance a lived-in world instead of a backdrop."

**The structure:** "Each chapter opens with one of Olive's research hypotheses,
doubling as thematic scene-setting." And "Chapter one opens mid-kiss."

### 2. What the prose refuses to do
Refuses to let the power imbalance be ignored — and, crucially, refuses to punish
refusal. The study notes that the author "doesn't pretend it doesn't exist" and
that the plot "actively addresses" it; and separately, that Adam is "determined to
have Olive's consent at every step of the way."

### 3. Character psychology
**The asymmetrical fall.** He has been in love with her for longer than she knows,
and he shows it in *actions* rather than words — "expressed through actions rather
than words."

**The restricted POV doing structural work.** The analysis is precise: "Because we
never get Adam's perspective, the reader experiences the 'feelings landing
unequally' beat exactly the way Olive does — including the moment she thinks
she's overheard confessing love for someone else, and he draws the same wrong
conclusion."

### 4. Most transferable mechanism
**The misread that the reader sees through and the protagonist doesn't.** Three
things at once: it makes the reader feel clever, it extends the slow burn, and it
generates the comedy.

**Where it breaks:** the corpus's criticism is sharp and we must inherit it:
"the author falls into the trap of making all narrative conflicts solvable with
'good communication that characters refuse to have,' an exhausting device that
artificially stretches the drama." And: "Adam is perfect to the point of
caricature... while Olive makes decisions no third-year PhD candidate would make,
sacrificing character credibility for plot convenience."

### Adopted
- The asymmetric fall (`bible/10` §2.2).
- Banter anchored to what the POV wants (`bible/10` §3.3).
- The restricted POV as structural engine (`bible/10` §2.2).
- Consent-forward design (`bible/10` §3.4).
- Opening mid-action ("Chapter one opens mid-kiss") (`bible/04` §3).

### Not adopted
- The withheld-communication device. Our conflicts resolve through **cost**, not
  through refusal to talk.
- The flawless love interest. The house requires a full six-part engine for every
  party, and "perfect" is a contradiction with no tension in it.

## B9 · *The Serpent and the Wings of Night* — Carissa Broadbent

### 1. Prose contribution
Short-sentenced and punchy, and the reception notices it as a *choice*: "the first
thing I noticed is that Broadbent loves to use short sentences. While there's
nothing inherently wrong with them, they can seem choppy." The house's dialect
takes the short sentence as a tool for pressure (see `bible/02` §2) and rejects the
choppiness — a burst of short sentences is rhythm; a page of them is a stutter.

The prose's other documented strength is **sensory grounding in the action**, using
all five senses "wonderfully, particularly in her battle scenes," while the
reception finds the *place* descriptions thinner than the action. That is the
house's own distinction, precisely stated: the world is better rendered through
what happens in it than through what it looks like.

And the worldbuilding is explicitly praised for *not* explaining: "She doesn't
over-explain the seven Houses, the history of Nyaxia, or the political tensions
simmering beneath every interaction. Instead, she embeds worldbuilding
organically, revealing information only when it matters."

### 2. What the prose refuses to do
**It refuses the safe ending.** This is the corpus's most valuable single finding
on the romantasy side and it revises a common assumption:

> The genre's HEA is a **series-level** contract, not a **book-level** one. *The
> Serpent and the Wings of Night* ends its first book on a betrayal, a death, and
> a forced political marriage — lovers-turned-enemies ruling over a burning city —
> and it was still a large commercial success, and the ending was praised as "one
> of the best one-two-three gut-punches in recent romantasy."

`bible/10` §6.3 has been amended accordingly.

### 3. Character psychology
**Shared trauma as the engine of intimacy, without romance as the consolation.**
The relationship is "built on shared trauma and the quiet understanding of two
predators who are tired of being hunted" — and the analysis notes that Broadbent
"ensures that the romance never undermines Oraya's individual agency."

The other finding: **the lover is not the prize.** The book is praised for the
readers' interest in the father–daughter relationship over the romantic one — which
is a striking structural choice, and a useful one: it means the romance's absence
in the first half is a *choice about where the reader looks*, not a defect.

And the systemic frame: the Kejari is "not simply a contest of strength, but a
mechanism of control that reinforces hierarchy and fear," and the gods "use mortal
suffering as entertainment." The protagonist's arc ends not with a win but with a
**refusal of the game's terms** — "Instead of asking for strength or immortality,
she asks for [his] life."

### 4. Most transferable mechanism
**The arena as a proximity machine.** A structure that forces two people into
sustained, dangerous, repeated proximity under conditions where neither can leave
— and that lets the romance run for hundreds of pages without either party having
to make a decision about it. The in-story time matters: "the structure of the plot
takes place over months, giving our two characters a believable amount of time to
work together."

**Where it breaks:** the same structure can feel mechanical if the trials don't
also change the characters. The reception notes that the supporting cast "didn't
[get] to develop... it wasn't until the end of the novel where the conflict brought
out their dimension."

### Adopted
- The series-level HEA contract (`bible/10` §6.3, amended).
- The arena/tournament as a proximity machine (`bible/10` §2).
- Refusing the game's terms as the arc's climax (`bible/10` §5.1).
- Organic worldbuilding that reveals only what matters (`bible/08`).
- The romance not undermining the protagonist's agency (`bible/10` §5).

### Not adopted
- The uniformly short sentence as a default register (`bible/02` §2 — bursts only).
- The love interest left "blander than the premise deserves for a long stretch."
  Our rule stands: both parties get a full six-part engine from the first chapter
  (`samples/01`).

## Appendix: the romantasy field, as a unit

### 1. Prose contribution
The field's prose converges on a recognisable temperature — lush, sensory, immediate
— and the corpus's critical consensus is that this is both the genre's commercial
engine and its most-cited weakness.

The commercial engine: romantasy's growth has been exceptional. Bloomberg estimates
$610M in 2024 (up from $454M in 2023); Circana BookScan reported 51M print romance
units in the US in the trailing twelve months, up 24% year over year; *Onyx
Storm*'s 2.7M first week was the fastest-selling adult fiction title in 20 years.
And the growth is concentrating in the **darker** registers — "dark romance,
paranormal romance, and anti-hero narratives are the fastest-growing subcategories."

### 2. What the prose refuses to do
The field increasingly refuses the light register, and that's a craft opportunity:
our house style is *already* dark-adjacent. The dial position 4–6 range is where
this house is strongest and the market is heading.

### 3. Character psychology
The field's most interesting psychological development is the **consent-forward
redesign of the dark romance**: the genre's most-cited problem (the
romanticising of coercive dynamics) is being solved structurally by giving the
love interest explicit agency and giving the reader a clear statement of the
relationship's terms.

### 4. Most transferable mechanism
**Trope concentration as a commercial fact and a craft fact.** Enemies-to-lovers
appears in roughly two-thirds of the top titles by shelf count. A romantasy that
wants to compete should pick its trope deliberately and play it honestly, and
differentiate on depth.

### Adopted
- One trope, played honestly (`bible/10` §7).
- Differentiation in depth rather than trope count.
- The dark register as the house's competitive position.

### Not adopted
- The field's assumption that trope density is the product. It is the *door*; the
  book is what's behind it.

## Adjacent case study · *The Stormlight Archive* — Brandon Sanderson

*Outside the twenty, because its magic is the plot's engine rather than its cost.
Analysed because it is the corpus's most instructive counter-example.*

### 1. Prose contribution
Sanderson supplies the term the house uses for its own default: **window-pane
prose**.

> "Some prose is like a stained glass window, where the window itself is
> beautiful irrespective of what you can see through it, whereas he aims for a
> clear, clean, transparent window that exists only to show you what's beyond. At
> its best, his prose falls away until you're hardly even aware that you're
> reading."

That is our house position, stated by a much larger-selling author, using a better
metaphor than ours. `bible/01` now uses it.

### 2. What the prose refuses to do
Sanderson refuses the ornamental, and the reception notes the cost of the
refusal: "The witty dialogue lacks wit." And "some things that bug me" lists
"an uneven prose style" alongside a critique of the philosophical content: the
complaint that "Brandon literally writes out the philosophical points he's
discussing in the book."

That is the lecture antipattern (`bible/11` E6) appearing in the most successful
prose in the genre — a useful demonstration that the failure is not a matter of
talent but of intention.

### 3. Character psychology
The corpus's most detailed account of **mental illness rendered accurately**
rather than as a trope:

- **Kaladin** is written as depression rather than as "tragic backstory" — "the
  mornings where getting up feels impossible, the voice quietly insisting everyone
  would be better off without you, the slow crush of believing it's your job to
  please or even save everyone and then losing people anyway." Sanderson has
  confirmed the seasonal-affective-disorder reading directly.
- **Shallan** is written with dissociative identity, and — this is the finding
  worth having — **she is reintegrated, not cured.** "Shallan doesn't 'cure'
  herself by deleting the alters. She *reintegrates*, learns to hold all three as
  parts of one whole person rather than warring strangers. Recovery as
  integration, not erasure."

That is precisely the house's position on trauma (`bible/06` §5, `bible/10` §5.1),
and it is the correct outcome of a rule stated as *"the romance must not cure the
wound."*

- **Szeth** is a "Extreme Doormat" whose honour compels him to serve whoever holds
  his oaths — a pure study of the house's Lie as a character engine.

### 4. Most transferable mechanism
**The structural contradiction as a virtue.** Kaladin's arc is widely described as
circular — he ends up roughly where he started — and the reception's own verdict is
well judged: "That's absolutely fine. These are all changes." A character arc that
isn't a line from A to B is a character arc about *integration*, not
*transformation*.

**Where it breaks:** the reception also notes repetition across volumes — "No one
ever gets tired of Shallan lying or Kaladin stepping back from combat" — and the
specific critique that "Sanderson seems allergic to friction between characters on
the 'right side.'" The house's antipattern A11 and D1 cover both.

### Adopted
- The term **window-pane prose** (`bible/01`).
- Recovery as integration, not erasure (`bible/06` §5.1, `bible/10` §5.1).
- Mental illness rendered as behaviour, not as backstory (`bible/06` §5).
- The circular arc as a legitimate shape (`bible/06` §2).

### Not adopted
- Stating the theme explicitly ("Brandon literally writes out the philosophical
  points"). `bible/11` E6.
- The structural permutation of the magic system as the plot's engine — a
  different design brief from ours.
- The alternating five-part structure with interludes: elegant, and it depends on
  a book length our house won't sustain.

## Cross-series synthesis: the five convergences

| # | Finding | Where it lives in the bible |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Prose is an instrument.** The strongest books are invisible; the authors who said so meant it. | `bible/01`, `samples/00` §12 |
| 2 | **POV is a lens.** Each POV notices different things and the narration changes. | `bible/05`, `samples/01` |
| 3 | **Consequence over complication.** Tension comes from costs landing. | Law VI, `bible/04` §2 |
| 4 | **Psychology is behaviour.** The writers who work describe less. | `bible/06` §3 |
| 5 | **Violence is a ledger.** Wounds are tracked; deaths remove people. | `bible/09` |

**And the one divergence that matters most:** the romantasy corpus runs hotter but
uses the same architecture. Which is why this bible has one house with two
temperature settings, not two houses.
