# The Fantasy-Prose Prose Bible

**Version 1.0 · Master reference**

> *"The best stories are those where you don't notice the richness of prose."*
> — Michael J. Sullivan, on writing *The Riyria Revelations* (his own stated aim was **invisibility**)

---

## 0. What we are building

We are writing adult fantasy in the low-magic / gritty / realist register — the lineage
of *A Song of Ice and Fire*, *The First Law*, *Realm of the Elderlings*, *The
Gentlemen Bastards* — **and** we are building the same house style's sibling for
romantasy, where the romance is load-bearing rather than decorative.

Two failure modes define what we're avoiding:

- **The porridge register.** Words chosen for their warmth rather than their accuracy.
  "Breathtaking," "ethereal," "ravishing," "mysterious." The reader is being told
  what to feel instead of being given something to feel about.
- **The grimdark cosplay.** Gore for texture, misery for authenticity, a cast of
  uniformly gray protagonists who all want the same thing and feel the same way about
  everything. Grimdark is not "everyone is sad." Grimdark is "the world has costs and
  the characters know it."

We want the third thing: **prose that is plain on the surface and exact underneath.**

The single sentence that governs this document:

> **Plain words, carried by an accurate mind.**

## 1. The Ten Laws

These are the load-bearing rules. Everything else in `bible/` elaborates them.

### Law I — Specificity is the whole job
Not "a dagger," but "a bread knife with the tip ground down, the edge dulled by
six months of skinning." Not "she was angry," but the three physical facts that
constitute anger for *this* person, right now, in this body.

> The word "anger" is a conclusion. The evidence for anger is a scene. Give the
> evidence and the reader will reach the conclusion faster than you could state it —
> and will trust you more for having made them do the work.

**Test:** If you can swap two details between two different scenes without the scene
breaking, the details are decorative. Cut them.

---

### Law II — Every character is a lens, not a narrator
Point of view is not a camera position. It is a **filter**: it decides what gets
noticed.

A farrier looking at a courtyard notices the horses' hooves, the state of the gelding's
teeth, whether a stranger's boots have been resoled recently. A bureaucrat notices who
is standing near whom. A grieving person notices nothing at all and is embarrassed
about it.

> Abercrombie's most legible and most borrowed technique is not plot or violence. It
> is that each of his POV characters carries a different *attention*, and the
> narration changes to match. A blunt, earthy northern captain does not notice the
> filigree on a ceiling. A torturer notices everything, because looking is all he can
> do. This is why "you can tell which character you're in from the first paragraph,
> with no identifying information."

**Test:** Read any paragraph of a POV chapter. Ask: *what would this person notice
that nobody else would?* If the answer is "nothing," rewrite the paragraph from
someone who would notice.

---

### Law III — The narration must know less than the writer
Every POV character holds a partial, biased, often wrong model of the world. The prose
is written *from inside that model*, not from above it.

Three consequences, all load-bearing:

1. **Belief, not truth.** When a character is certain and wrong, the prose is certain
   and wrong with them. "He was certain she had been lying" — not "she had been
   lying."
2. **Wrongness must be costless to the character.** Characters don't say "I might be
   wrong." They say "I am sure." The reader carries the gap.
3. **The gap between two POVs is free conflict.** Two characters can describe the
   *same scene* and disagree about what happened. This is the cheapest, strongest
   source of tension in serialised fantasy.

> The best serial fiction runs two POV streams against each other: the reader knows
> more than any one character, so no character is ever in the position to explain the
> plot to the reader.

**Test:** In your last chapter, find one sentence where the narrator knows something
the POV character could not know. Delete or relocate it.

---

### Law IV — Dialogue is the instrument; narration is the setup
Roughly, in the studied corpus, **half to two-thirds of the words on the page are
dialogue**. The narration exists to get us into the room, to hold what we can't say,
and to land the turn at the end.

Dialogue must do at least two of five jobs simultaneously:

| Job | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Advance** | Changes who knows what, or what the situation is |
| **Characterise** | Reveals the speaker's diction, cadence, education, evasion |
| **Subtext** | Says one thing, means another; the gap is the point |
| **Pressure** | Forces the other party to reveal, concede, or evade |
| **Texture** | Establishes register, class, region, profession, relationship history |

The failure mode is **decoration dialogue**: lines that could be exchanged by any two
characters in any novel. Test: remove the speaker tags. Could another character have
said this? Then it isn't dialogue yet — it's a speech.

> The genre benchmark for this is Abercrombie's Glokta and Logen: two people talking,
> both saying exactly what they mean, and the reader hearing everything neither says.
> No subtext required. The characterisation *is* the subtext.

**Test:** Find a line of dialogue that could be moved to any other book without
editing. It is not finished.

---

### Law V — Violence has a ledger
Every injury is recorded and every debt is collected.

- A cut hand means a hand that doesn't grip the same on page 300.
- A death means a hole in the cast, a changed inheritance, a grief scene that will be
  *written*, and a promise to the reader that this person mattered.
- Sexual violence is never a spectacle; it is a **wound with consequences** that
  other characters will react to, sometimes wrongly.

This is where the studied corpus diverges sharply from most fantasy, and where
grit earns its name. In *The Faithful and the Fallen*, violence is never a trophy and
grief never resolves neatly. In *Realm of the Elderlings*, a protagonist who kills
someone he recognises stops to think about their shared memories. **The detail after
the killing is the actual scene.**

**Test:** For every wound you have inflicted on the cast, is there a later page where
it changes someone's behaviour? If not, it's set dressing.

---

### Law VI — Consequence over complication
The studied corpus does not get its tension from plot complexity. It gets it from
**costs landing**.

A character makes a reasonable decision. The decision is reasonable *given what they
know and believe*. Then the world charges them. That gap — between the character's
model and reality — is the engine.

> Abercrombie's plots are best described as *causality over prophecy*: who has
> leverage, who is exhausted, who is lying. Martin's "anything can happen" is not
> randomness, it is the *absence of plot armour* — consequence is universal, so the
> reader never knows who is safe.

**Test:** In your last chapter, did any character pay for what they did? If every
character left the scene whole, you wrote a wish fulfilment.

---

### Law VII — The world is a set of pressures, not a manual
Exposition is delivered **through people who want something**. The only acceptable
forms of worldbuilding are:

1. **Someone wants it.** A character needs a rule, a history, a debt, a border drawn.
2. **Someone resents it.** The world is shown by what it obliges people to endure.
3. **Someone gets it wrong.** The rules are stated by a person with an agenda, which
   means we learn the rule *and* the agenda simultaneously.
4. **Someone pays for it.** Cost is the fastest route to comprehension.

> The single most common failure in the studied corpus is not bad prose. It is the
> **info-dump**: a character reciting the history of a nation, or the genealogies of
> five houses, to a listener who didn't ask. The studied books earn their
> worldbuilding the hard way — through dialogue where somebody wants something, through
> consequences, through the incidental detail of a place.

**Test:** Every paragraph of exposition: *who wants this information, and what do they
get for it?* If nobody wants it, cut it.

---

### Law VIII — Memory is a character with a grudge
In the serious register, a character's history is not backstory. It is a **live
presence** that colours perception, corrupts interpretation, and shapes choice.

Techniques, in ascending strength:

- **The wrong memory.** The character misremembers, and the prose reports the memory
  as fact. The reader feels the error without being told.
- **The involuntary cue.** A smell, a posture, a phrase triggers a flinch the
  character didn't consent to.
- **The behavioural residue.** Trauma changes *what people do* — hypervigilance,
  the flinch at being touched, the compulsive joke, the inability to accept
  kindness. The prose renders this as habit, not as backstory.
- **The public lie.** The character tells a version of their past that has become
  load-bearing, and the prose lets us watch the lie work on other people.

> This is the single most transferable technique in the whole study. It is how *The
> First Law* makes you sympathise with a torturer and a murderer, and how *Realm of
> the Elderlings* turns a character's childhood abuse into a permanent lens on
> every relationship that follows.

**Test:** Does the character's past change anything on *this* page? If not, it's a
prop.

---

### Law IX — The turn, not the summary
Chapters end on a **turn**: a reversal, a revelation, a decision, a new fact that
reframes what came before. Not a summary of feelings, not a fade-out.

This is a craft inheritance from screenwriting, and it is the single highest-leverage
habit in serial fantasy. A chapter that ends on a turn makes the reader physically
turn the page; a chapter that ends on a summary makes the reader set the book down.

> "George Martin has said that his screenwriting background taught him to end each
> chapter on a tense or revelational moment, a twist or a cliffhanger — a TV act
> break." That's not a gimmick. It's the correct unit of suspense.

**Test:** Last sentence of the chapter. Does it *change* anything? If it only
recaps, rewrite the last two paragraphs.

---

### Law X — Earn the ending of the sentence
Every chapter, every scene, every book ends on a line that is **satisfied**. Not
resolved — satisfied. The prose closes. The last sentence sounds like something a
person would say, not like a paragraph of notes.

> A useful test from *The First Law*'s own construction: characters in that book end
> their lines on something concrete — a gesture, a drink, a door, a price. Logen ends
> a fight with a flat statement of fact. That's a chapter closing on satisfaction, not
> on summary.

**Test:** Read the last three sentences aloud. If they sound like an outline, they
need work.

## 2. The calibration dial

Not every book wants the same setting on this dial. **Set it before you draft.**

| Dial position | Prose temperature | Reference points |
|---|---|---|
| **0 — Cold realism** | Flat, documentary, close to the bone. Interiority suppressed. | *Realm of the Elderlings* (Farseer), *The Poppy War* |
| **2 — House default** | Plain surface, exact underneath. Dry humour permitted. Violence lands. | *The First Law*, *The Green Bone Saga* |
| **4 — Lush & dark** | Sensory abundance, gothic atmosphere, heightened colour. | *A Court of Thorns and Roses*, *One Dark Window* |
| **6 — Romantasy heat** | Romance is load-bearing. Interiority is explicit. Tension is the engine. | *The Empyrean*, *The Love Hypothesis* |
| **8 — Poetic** | Metaphor-forward, rhythmic, mythic register. Use sparingly. | *The Second Apocalypse*, *Earthsea* |
| **10 — Mythic** | Ballad, epic, scripture. Reserved for a handful of lines per book. | *Discworld* (at full comic), *Malazan* (at full bombast) |

**House rule:** A book may move along the dial *within itself*, but the shift must be
motivated by POV, not by the author's mood. A chapter that suddenly becomes lush
because the prose got excited is a bug.

## 3. Quick reference card

```
NARRATION
  · Filter every sentence through the POV's attention
  · Belief, not truth — write what they think is happening
  · No narrator knowledge the POV lacks
  · Cut any adverb that isn't doing structural work

SENTENCES
  · Vary length deliberately: a short one lands a long one
  · Prefer concrete nouns to abstract ones
  · One image per sentence. Two images fight.
  · Anglo-Saxon first (work, stone, blood, cold, hunger) — Latinate only for the elite
  · Cut "very," "really," "quite," "somewhat," "in a way that"
  · No "heart pounded," "eyes widened," "shivers down spine"
  · Window-pane prose: the glass is clean, the pane is thin, the reader looks through

DIALOGUE
  · Two jobs minimum per line
  · Vary the tag: said / asked / nothing / an action
  · Never tag with an emotion ("she said sadly")
  · Subtext by omission, not by euphemism

WORLD
  · Every fact of worldbuilding must be wanted by someone
  · Cost > explanation
  · Names are the last info, not the first

CHARACTER
  · Want / Need / Wound / Lie / Fear / Contradiction — fill all six
  · The contradiction is the character
  · Their history must change this page

VIOLENCE
  · Mechanism, then consequence, then aftermath
  · No gloss, no glamour shot
  · The ledger appears later

CHAPTER
  · Open in motion, or on a line that costs something
  · Turn at the end
  · Last line satisfies
```

## 4. How to use this bible

**If you're drafting:** keep `bible/01` and `bible/04` open. Write ugly first. Diagnose
later.

**If you're revising a chapter:** work `bible/12-chapter-diagnostic.md` top to bottom.
It is a pass/fail instrument, not a checklist of feelings.

**If you're building a cast:** use `samples/01-voice-sheets.md` as the template, and
read `bible/06-character-psychology.md`.

**If you're building a world:** `bible/08-worldbuilding-in-prose.md`.

**If you're writing the romance arc:** `bible/10-romantasy-mode.md`.

**If you want to know why a rule exists:** `research/02-series-notes.md` has the
per-series findings; `research/03-sources.md` has the links.

## 5. The one thing to remember

The studied corpus disagrees about almost everything — structure, magic systems, tone,
how many books the series should have. Abercrombie writes fast and functional;
Bakker writes slow and scriptural; Maas is lush; Sullivan is invisible; Hobb is
mythic-simple; Kuang has changed her whole style between books and considers both
valid.

What they agree on, across every one of them:

> **The prose exists to make a reader believe a person was there.**

Not to sound impressive. Not to impress. To make someone believe a person was there,
with a body, in the weather, with a reason to be saying what they said.

Everything in this bible is downstream of that.
