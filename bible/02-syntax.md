# 02 · Syntax

**How we build sentences.**

> *"Prose rhythms are regular, if at all, only on a very much larger, longer scale.
> And they're hard to talk about, since we have no vocabulary for them."*
> — Ursula K. Le Guin, on revising (*Navigating the Ocean of Story*)

Le Guin's method is the right one and it is worth imitating: **read it aloud**.
Wherever your reading aloud hesitates, there is a fault in the rhythm.

---

## 1. The sentence shape we default to

The workhorse of this house:

```
[Subject] + [verb, present tense] + [object] + [qualifier]
```

Short. Front-loaded. Action before analysis.

> ✅ The horse took the fence badly and Vorn went over the near side.
>
> ✅ She put the ledger face down and pushed it across the table.

Why this shape:

1. **It survives translation.** (Note: *Game of Thrones* was translated into dozens of
   languages; Martin's plain Germanic vocabulary was part of why it travelled.)
2. **It's read faster**, which matters in serial fiction where the reader is
   negotiating with a phone.
3. **It doesn't demonstrate cleverness.** Cleverness belongs in the *choice* of
   detail, not in the structure of the sentence.

**When to break the shape:** when the break itself is the information. The rule is
never "short sentences are good." The rule is: *the reader should feel the shape
change.*

---

## 2. Length variation: the only rhythm tool that matters

Read a page of good prose aloud. What you hear is not uniform sentences; it is
**bursts of one length interrupted by another.**

| Purpose | Length | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Establishing | Long | "The room had been built for men who liked walls, low ceilings, and the particular smell of tallow and old wool, and Vorn had never once in eleven years decided that he was comfortable in it." |
| Turning | Short | "He wasn't." |
| Grounding | Long | "The horse took the fence badly and Vorn went over the near side." |
| Impact | Very short | "Vorn didn't move." |
| Aftermath | Long | "The man in the grey coat walked back to his horse without hurry, and unhitched it, and led it out through the gap in the hedge where the ditch had swallowed the road, and by the time he reached the field he had stopped looking back." |

**The three-beat turn.** The most reliable shape in the corpus for delivering bad
news: **setup (long) → turn (short) → consequence (long).**

> "Maren had been counting the days since the fever broke, which was thirty-one, and
> she had a system for it now, a mark on the inside of her wrist for every week, and
> she was working out the marks for the twelfth when her mother came in without
> knocking. Eight. There were eight left.
>
> "She set the knife down very carefully, edge away, the way you set down anything
> that might get away from you, and she did not look at her mother's face, because
> she knew what her mother's face was doing, and she could not stand to see it being
> kind."

That's a long setup, a one-word turn, and a long consequence. It is the shape of most
of the best moments in the studied corpus.

## 3. The two hard prohibitions

### 3.1 No two images per sentence

A sentence can carry one image. Two images compete and the reader has to hold both
while also holding the syntax.

> ❌ "His boots rang on the frozen mud like a funeral drum, and the mist curled
> around him like a shroud."
>
> ✅ "His boots rang on the frozen mud. The mist came up to his knees."

Two similes in one sentence also means the writer chose a mood twice, which means the
sentence is doing the job of two sentences badly.

**Rule:** one metaphor per sentence, and no metaphor in a sentence that also contains
a piece of dialogue.

### 3.2 No adverb doing a verb's job

> ❌ "She quickly closed the ledger."
>
> ✅ "She closed the ledger and put it under her hand."

If the adverb is doing real work, the verb should do it instead. Most adverbs can be
deleted and the sentence improves:

> ❌ "He carefully, quietly, and slowly lowered the lantern."
>
> ✅ "He lowered the lantern. The dark came in faster than he expected."

**House rule:** no more than **one** adverb per paragraph, and no adverb at all in a
sentence that opens a scene.

The exceptions: adverbs of degree on dialogue verbs ("said quietly"), adverbs of
frequency ("often," "never"), and adverbs that specify a *degree* that has no
verbal equivalent ("very," "slightly" — which are, in truth, usually signs of a
missing verb).

## 4. Filters

A **filter** is a verb that reports the act of perceiving rather than the thing
perceived.

> ❌ "She saw that the door was open."
> ❌ "He noticed the smell of smoke."
> ❌ "She realised that he was lying."

Filters are almost always a symptom of a POV that isn't yet a *person*. A person
doesn't "realise" — they think, or they don't, and the prose shows what they think
about.

**The rewrite ladder:**

1. Delete the filter. "The door was open."
2. If the filter was doing POV work, convert it to an interpretation: "The door was
   open. It shouldn't have been."
3. If it was doing *the reader's* work — i.e. the information is genuinely new —
   keep it, but only for the first instance. Second mention of the same fact can be
   filtered; first mention should not be.

**Legitimate filters** (rare, and deliberate): when the *noticing* is the point.
A character who has been numb for pages and suddenly *notices the light* — that
filter is characterisation. Use it once, and let it land.

## 5. Free indirect discourse: the best tool in the house

Free indirect discourse (FID) blends the character's voice into the narration
without quotation marks or tags. It's the technique that lets a close-third
narrator sound like a person rather than a camera.

> "He could see the girl was lying. *Nobody* lied with their hands like that. His
> mother had lied with her hands like that for eleven years, and he had learned the
> difference at some point he couldn't remember."

The italicised clause is the character's *voice* intruding into the narration. It
carries contempt, and it carries a memory, and it does both without a sentence
explaining the contempt.

**When to use it:** when the character's *judgement* is more interesting than the
event — and always when the judgement is *wrong*. FID is how you render a biased
mind without making it explicit.

**How to signal it:** italics for a short intrusion; a shift in vocabulary that only
this character would use; a grammatical free-for-all that the neutral narration
wouldn't commit to.

**The trap:** FID that isn't marked reads as a grammatical error. If more than one
sentence in a paragraph is FID without a marker, the effect is lost. One intrusion
per paragraph.

**Note from the corpus:** Ursula Le Guin's *Earthsea* used this technique extensively
— the narrator's sympathy with the protagonist is achieved *structurally*, by blurring
the line between narration and character feeling, rather than by declaring the
narrator's sympathy. Jordan did the same thing with *Wheel of Time*, where the
narrative colour is dictated by POV: a blacksmith notices the quality of metalwork in
someone's tools; a soldier notices whether a sword has been cleaned.

**Note from the corpus, negative:** Abercrombie is *explicit* about this. He gives
one character (the torturer) an italicised interior monologue throughout, and
another (the northern captain) a coarse dialect, and another (the colour-blind
swordswoman) short staccato sentences with long paragraphs and emphasised single
lines — **and no colour words at all in her descriptive passages**, because she cannot
see colour, which flattens her emotional register too. This is FID and register
doing psychological work simultaneously. It's the gold standard for making a narrator
sound like a person.

## 6. Syntax for violence

Violence is fast. It is short sentences, active verbs, and no subordinate clauses.
This is not a stylistic choice; it's a reading-speed requirement. The reader's
attention must not snag.

> ❌ "Although the man had been larger, and stronger, she drove the short blade
> upward and, as he folded, she twisted it, which was a thing she had practised."
>
> ✅ "He was bigger than her. She drove the blade up under the sternum and twisted it,
> which she had practised."

Subordinate clauses during violence create the sense that the writer is *watching*
rather than *in*. Cut the "although," "as," "because." Keep the coordination.

The aftermath is the opposite: long sentences, subordinate clauses, the full weight
of what just happened arriving. **Short in the act, long in the cost.**

## 7. Syntax for romance

The mechanics of a romantic moment require one specific syntactic move: **the
sentence must contain both people at once.**

> ✅ "He was careful with her, the way you are careful with something that is going
> to break, and she noticed the carefulness, and she did not say thank you, because
> saying it would have named it."

> ❌ "He touched her gently. She felt a shiver run down her spine. His heart
> hammered."

The second version has three separate reactions and no relationship between them. The
first has one action, one observation, and one choice — and the choice is the
character.

**The load-bearing syntax of romance is the shared clause:** a subordinate structure
where the second character is grammatically inside the first character's action.
"He said it as though he meant it" / "she answered before he could finish." That
construction *is* intimacy on the page.

**Pacing note:** the studied romantasy hits their marks with an em-dash or an
ellipsis before the turn — a small syntactic hitch that mimics the held breath. It
works. It is also the single most over-used device in the genre, and in this house it
is capped at **one per scene**.

## 8. The list of things we do not do

| Don't | Do instead |
|---|---|
| Two images in one sentence | Two sentences |
| "Realized," "noticed," "saw that" | The fact, or the interpretation |
| Subordinate clauses in action | Coordination |
| Emotion-nouns | Behaviour |
| Adverb-laden verbs | Better verbs |
| "Although/While" as openers | Start at the second clause |
| Two adverbs in a row | One, or none |
| Sentence-length uniformity | Burst-and-turn |
| "It was not X. It was Y." construction | Simply Y |
| A metaphor in a dialogue beat | Concrete action |

The "It was not X, it was Y" construction deserves its own note: it is a strong
rhythm and it is *also* a tic. Two per chapter is a pattern; five is a tic. It also
tends to smuggle in a claim the prose hasn't earned.

---

## 9. Self-check

Read the chapter aloud. Then:

1. **Hesitations** — where did you stumble? (rhythm fault)
2. **Fat** — which sentences could lose a clause and lose nothing?
3. **Two images** — any sentence where you can see two pictures at once?
4. **Filters** — count "saw," "noticed," "realised," "felt," "seemed," "watched."
5. **Adverbs** — count. Halve them.
6. **The turn** — is there one sentence in the chapter where the shape changes
   sharply? If not, the chapter has no pulse.
