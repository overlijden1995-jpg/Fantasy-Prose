# 09 · Violence & the Body

**Writing harm that costs something.**

> *"The violence is fumbly and messy and not beautiful or skilful; it's horrible,
> instantaneous and unpleasant, and I wanted the sex to feel honest and earthy in
> the same kind of way."*
> — Joe Abercrombie, on *The First Law*

That is the whole doctrine. Fumbly, messy, not beautiful, and *consequential*.

---

## 1. Why this matters

Grit is not gore. Gore without consequence is a shock image, and it fades. The
studied corpus's violence works because of the **ledger**: the wound appears again,
the scar changes a grip, the death changes an inheritance, the grief becomes a
scene.

**The rule that separates the two:**

> **Gore is what the reader sees. Grit is what the reader carries.**

---

## 2. The three-part anatomy

Every significant act of violence in this house has three parts, in this order:

### Part 1 — Mechanism
How it happens, physically, with the specificity that makes it real.

- **No abstraction.** "He stabbed him" is abstraction. "He put the blade in under the
  sternum and leaned on it, because that was the only way that worked on a coat
  like that" is mechanism.
- **Physical truth.** A sword through a coat is not a sword through a body. Armour
  turns blades. A knife in a ribcage at a bad angle does nothing interesting.
- **The body's resistance.** Weight, surprise, the way a body is heavier than you
  expect and then suddenly isn't.

### Part 2 — Consequence
The immediate result, rendered as change, not as commentary.

- Bleeding is a *rate*, not a colour. Someone bleeds fast and someone bleeds slow
  and both of them keep walking for a while.
- The wound appears on the page again in a later chapter, and it's worse or better
  than it was.
- A broken arm is a *broken arm* for the rest of the book. It's in the way.

### Part 3 — Aftermath
The scene after, and it must be a different scene.

- The person who did it, alone, or with someone who won't say anything.
- The person who watched it.
- The practical business: the body, the washing, the debt, the telling of
  relatives.

**The classic error:** merging Part 3 into Part 1. The character kills someone and
*in the same breath* reflects on it. That reflection is a lie — nobody processes
murder in the four seconds after it. The processing comes later, in a different
scene, usually with different company.

> In *Realm of the Elderlings*, a protagonist who kills someone he recognises
> stops and thinks about their shared memories. That's not the same paragraph — the
> kill happens, the prose moves on, and *later*, in its own space, the recognition
> arrives.

## 3. The set-piece problem

The corpus's most-cited weakness, and the one most worth avoiding:

**Set-piece writing** is planning a book around a sequence of dynamic scenes
imagined in advance and transcribed brain-to-page. It produces set pieces that are
*cool* and have no relationship to the story.

The corpus's strengths are the opposite:
- The scene serves the character, not the reverse.
- Action is written from inside a person's fear and attention, not from outside.
- The blow-by-blow is *short*.

### 3.1 Combat as psychology

A fight scene should reveal who these people are. The best fights in the corpus
are, functionally, **portraits**:

- Abercrombie's Glokta fights from a wheelchair and it changes every exchange.
- Gwynne's fights are written by a man with hands-on weapon training and the
  specificity is the point — but the *cost* is the point too: the warriors who win
  are the ones who had something to lose.
- Fonda Lee, a martial artist, writes the *jade* — the magic that amplifies intent —
  as a direct expression of the fighter's character. The magic system is the
  psychology.

**Test:** remove the fight and write one line about each fighter. If the line says
nothing the fight didn't say, the fight was a set piece.

### 3.2 Length

Violence in this house is **fast**: short sentences, active verbs, no subordinate
clauses (see `02-syntax.md` §6). The prose slows *after*.

Typical ratio: a fight that takes two pages of story time should take about a page
of prose, and be followed by half a page of aftermath.

### 3.3 The competence rule

**Characters should not be better at fighting than the author has thought about.**
Gwynne, with reenactment experience, and Lee, with martial arts training, both
write fights that a practitioner could watch without wincing — and both were
explicitly praised for it.

**Practical rule:** you do not need expertise, you need *research done once and
used precisely*. Pick the three or four moves that matter, learn them properly, and
use those. Do not describe seventeen moves.

## 4. Death

### 4.1 The rules

1. **Deaths should be consequential and usually surprising.** The corpus's best
   deaths are of characters the reader cares about, at moments that reframe
   something earlier.
2. **No plot armour, but no arbitrary cruelty either.** In *A Song of Ice and Fire*,
   Martin does not kill characters for shock. Each death answers a question the book
   has been asking.
3. **The body is a logistical problem.** Someone has to move it, hide it, account
   for it, or explain it. That logistics is often the best scene.
4. **The death is not the scene. The absence is.** The scene that follows — the
   empty chair, the job that needs doing, the person who has to be told — is where
   the death actually happens.
5. **Named minor characters count.** If a character has a name and a function, their
   death hurts. If they don't, their death is weather.

### 4.2 The death of a POV character

If a POV character dies, the chapter they die in should be their last, and the
*next* chapter should be the world's, not the mourner's, or — better — the mourner's
but from someone else's eyes.

> The corpus's typical move is to **not give us the mourner's POV at all** for a
> while, and let us watch the world go on, wrongly, without them. It is brutal and
> it is exactly right.

## 5. Injury as characterisation

The most useful single device in the corpus.

An injury that **shapes behaviour** rather than decorating it:

| Injury | Behaviour it produces |
|---|---|
| Bad shoulder | Can't lift; has to be helped onto a horse; learns to ride one-handed |
| Lost fingers | Grip changes; a particular hold becomes impossible; adapts a tool |
| Lost sight in one eye | Depth perception problems; turns the head; drinks differently |
| Stiff leg | Rides badly; walks differently; is slower than the others and knows it |
| Nerve damage | Can't feel temperature; burns; hides it |

**The mechanism:**
1. The injury happens on the page, in detail.
2. Within three pages, it *appears* again as a practical problem.
3. Within thirty, it has become a **habit** — the way the character moves, sits,
   reaches, or refuses.
4. Other characters *notice*, and one of them says something about it that reveals
   how they feel about the person.

Step 4 is where the injury becomes characterisation rather than injury.

### 5.1 The colour-blind filter

A technique from Abercrombie's *First Law* worth stealing in principle (not
necessarily in execution): one of his POV characters is colour-blind, and **her
descriptive passages contain no colour words at all**. This does two things: it
makes the prose distinguishably hers, and it flattens her emotional register —
because she also can't describe the world in the terms that would convey mood. The
limitation *is* the psychology.

**Generalisation:** give each POV character **one perceptual limitation**, real or
invented, and render the world through it consistently.

## 6. Sexual violence

### 6.1 The rules

1. **Never as spectacle.** It is not a set piece. It is a wound.
2. **Never without consequence.** The consequences appear on the page later — in
   the way a character holds a cup, sleeps, or answers a question.
3. **Never for the reader's titillation.** The camera is not the POV's eyes. This is
   the hardest technical problem in the book and the corpus's solution is the
   solution: **render the aftermath, and render the person's withholding.**
4. **The survivors are not defined by it.** A survivor who is defined by their
   assault for the rest of the book has been reduced to an event. In the studied
   corpus, most survivors are defined by what they *do* next.
5. **Others react, and they react wrongly.** The best material in this area is
   other characters' badly-formed responses: the person who says nothing, the person
   who says too much, the person who wants to talk about it and can't, the person
   who is relieved and then ashamed of being relieved.

### 6.2 The technical method

The most effective technique in the corpus for handling this material is the
**structural ellipsis**: the event is not depicted. It is referred to, later,
obliquely, and its presence is felt in the prose through the *things the character
does and doesn't say*.

A character who has been assaulted and is being kind to someone else in the scene is
doing more work than any depiction would do.

> This is not avoidance. It is the same principle as the chapter ending on a turn:
> **the reader's own imagination is more powerful than your description**, and
> writing it out spends a resource you don't need to spend.

## 7. Torture, execution, and the body in extremis

The same three-part anatomy applies. The specific danger here is **aestheticisation**:
the prose becoming beautiful in a way that glorifies what's happening.

**The defence:** anchor the prose in the *practitioner*, not the victim, and keep
it *flat*.

Abercrombie's Glokta is a torturer whose interior monologue is precise,
professional, and utterly without lyricism. That flatness is the point: the horror
comes from the reader noticing that the man doing this is *competent* and
*untroubled*, and having to sit with that.

**Rule:** never let the narration enjoy it. If the prose sounds good, it's wrong.

## 8. Violence in the romantasy register

In romance-first fantasy, the same rules apply with two adjustments:

1. **The stakes of the violence are relational.** A wound to the heroine is a wound
   to the relationship. This changes what the scene is *about*.
2. **Sex and violence share a grammar.** Both are acts that change the body's
   ownership. Handle one with the same seriousness as the other.

**But:** the heat is doing plot work. If the sex scene doesn't change the power
structure between the characters, it isn't earning its place — and the corpus's
best intimacy scenes are the ones where the *stakes* of the scene change.

> "She had wanted him for eleven days and the wanting had been a kind of weather,
> and now the weather had broken and she was standing in the middle of it with no
> coat on and no idea what the next hour was going to be."

That sentence is doing more for the romance than a page of description.

## 9. Violence self-check

1. **Mechanism** — do I know how this actually works, physically?
2. **The three parts** — mechanism, consequence, aftermath all present?
3. **Separation** — is the reflection in a different scene from the act?
4. **The ledger** — will this wound appear again in thirty pages?
5. **Psychology** — does the fight tell us who these people are?
6. **Length** — is the prose short where it should be short?
7. **Compassion** — would I be willing to have the POV read this scene aloud?
8. **Cost to the reader** — is there anything here that only exists to be
   disturbing? (If yes, cut it. It will not be missed and it will be remembered.)
9. **Aestheticisation** — is the prose *enjoying* it anywhere?
