# 11 · Antipatterns

**The twenty ways this goes wrong.**

Each entry: the symptom, why it fails, where it comes from in the studied corpus,
and the fix.

---

## A. Prose-level

### A1. Two images in a sentence
**Symptom:** "His boots rang on the mud like a funeral drum, and the mist curled
around him like a shroud."
**Why it fails:** the reader must hold two pictures and one syntax. Both images
compete; neither lands. The writer chose a mood twice, which means one sentence is
doing two jobs badly.
**Fix:** two sentences. One image each. If the second image is essential, the first
one is decoration.

---

### A2. Filter verbs
**Symptom:** "She saw that the door was open." "He noticed the smell of smoke."
**Why it fails:** the POV isn't a person yet, it's a camera. Also, "she saw that"
is three words doing one word's work.
**Fix:** delete the filter, or convert the observation into the character's
interpretation. "The door was open. It shouldn't have been."

---

### A3. Adverb stacks
**Symptom:** "She quickly and nervously closed the ledger."
**Why it fails:** adverbs are the writer reporting their own carelessness. The verb
should carry the manner.
**Fix:** find the accurate verb. "She shut the ledger and pressed her thumb into the
spine until it creased."

---

### A4. Emotion-nouns instead of evidence
**Symptom:** "She felt a pang of grief." "He was overcome with longing."
**Why it fails:** it *tells* the reader, which is the one thing the reader is
capable of doing without us.
**Fix:** the behaviour, the cost, the small physical fact.

---

### A5. The "It was not X. It was Y." construction
**Symptom:** "It wasn't anger. It was something older than that."
**Why it fails:** it's a strong rhythm, and it smuggles in a claim the prose hasn't
earned. It also becomes a tic.
**Fix:** cut to the second clause. If the contrast is genuinely doing work, show
both halves.

---

### A6. Register drift
**Symptom:** a chapter written flat and controlled turns suddenly lyrical in a
paragraph of heightened feeling, and the reader feels the seam.
**Why it fails:** it reveals the author, not the character. The reader was
immersed in a person's voice and now hears a novelist.
**Fix:** find the most beautiful sentence in the chapter and ask whether it's
earned. Rewrite it at the scene's register.

---

### A7. The adjective pile
**Symptom:** "a devastatingly beautiful, heart-wrenching, unforgettable moment."
**Why it fails:** stacked evaluative adjectives are a writer shouting. Each one
diminishes the next.
**Fix:** one adjective, or none. The specificity is in the noun.

---

### A8. Em-dash overuse
**Symptom:** "He looked at her — and she knew — and neither of them spoke — and
the door opened —"
**Why it fails:** a device that signals intensity becomes a way of avoiding
sentence structure. The reader starts seeing the punctuation instead of the prose.
**Fix:** in this house, one em-dash per scene, and never two in adjacent sentences.

---

### A9. The adverb-as-adjective confusion
**Symptom:** "He was suddenly aware of the cold." "She felt completely safe."
**Why it fails:** the adverb modifies the character's state rather than the verb, so
it adds nothing to the verb.
**Fix:** "The cold was in his hands now." "She stopped checking the door."

---

### A10. The purple sentence
**Symptom:** three subordinate clauses, two metaphors, and a semicolon, in a
sentence about someone opening a door.
**Why it fails:** the reader has to do work before they get to the event. In serial
fiction, the event is the point.
**Fix:** the three-part anatomy from `09`. Mechanism → consequence → aftermath.
Each gets clean sentences.

---

## B. POV-level

### B1. Head-hopping mid-scene
**Symptom:** two paragraphs into someone's mind, then a paragraph into someone
else's, without a break.
**Why it fails:** the reader loses their footing and has to re-establish who they
are with. The intimacy of close third is destroyed.
**Fix:** break the scene, or commit to one consciousness. The mid-scene switch is a
special effect, not a default.

---

### B2. Narrator omniscience
**Symptom:** "She didn't know it yet, but he had already decided to leave."
**Why it fails:** it breaks the contract the moment it's broken. If the narrator
knows, the reader stops trusting the narrator.
**Fix:** "He had decided to leave. He was careful not to look at her while he
decided."

---

### B3. The POV who notices only plot-relevant things
**Symptom:** every detail the POV notices advances the plot or reveals character.
**Why it fails:** it's a plot with a person attached, not a person in a place.
**Fix:** give the POV an attention with no plot use. Let them notice the horses'
hooves.

---

### B4. The reliable moral arbiter
**Symptom:** the POV is right about everyone else, and the prose agrees with them.
**Why it fails:** the reader is being told what to think. It's the failure mode of
grimdark cosplay — grim *and* self-righteous.
**Fix:** the POV is wrong about something. Their judgement is a lens, not a verdict.

---

### B5. The rotating cast with no rotation
**Symptom:** nine POVs, and the reader could not tell you how any of them sound
different.
**Why it fails:** they aren't characters; they're chapter labels.
**Fix:** give each a different attention, vocabulary, and register. Do the
voice-sheet exercise in `samples/01`.

---

## C. Character-level

### C1. The label
**Symptom:** "a brooding, dangerous, tortured hero."
**Why it fails:** it's a list of marketing adjectives. The reader has been told
nothing.
**Fix:** one behaviour that demonstrates all three, in a specific circumstance.

---

### C2. The trauma as label
**Symptom:** "She was broken. Everything she touched turned to ash and her mother's
face swam up out of the fire and she screamed without a sound."
**Why it fails:** the trauma is a *descriptor*, not a cause. It's used to signal
"dark" rather than to explain a person.
**Fix:** the wound's *effect on behaviour* in the present. What does she do that
she wouldn't do if she hadn't been through it? Show that.

---

### C3. The two-trait character
**Symptom:** "He's a good man who has done terrible things."
**Why it fails:** two traits is a contradiction only if one of them is *specific*.
"Stoic and kind" is a demographic. "Loyal absolutely and has already betrayed them
twice" is a person.
**Fix:** the contradiction must be actionable — it must make different choices in
the same situation.

---

### C4. The competent-competent problem
**Symptom:** two characters who are both brilliant, both likeable, and both right.
**Why it fails:** there's no friction and no chemistry. The reader has nothing to
watch.
**Fix:** make one of them good at something the other is bad at, and make them *need*
it.

---

### C5. The villain without a reason
**Symptom:** a villain who is cruel because the plot requires an antagonist.
**Why it fails:** the reader can dismiss them, and dismissing them means
dismissing the threat.
**Fix:** the reason stated accurately, at least once, in their own voice,
un-undercut. They must be right about *something*.

---

### C6. The passive protagonist
**Symptom:** the POV reacts for four hundred pages and never chooses.
**Why it fails:** the reader is watching, not following. The corpus's best
protagonists — however flawed — *choose*, and the choices are wrong, and they pay.
**Fix:** in every scene, the POV makes a decision that costs something.

---

### C7. The passive-aggressive victim
**Symptom:** a character who suffers and is admired for suffering.
**Why it fails:** the book becomes about the author's feelings.
**Fix:** the survivor is defined by what they *do* next.

---

## D. World-level

### D1. The info-dump
**Symptom:** a character recites the history of five houses.
**Why it fails:** nobody wants it. The listener doesn't react. The reader can't
retain it.
**Fix:** the five channels in `08`. Cut it and see if anyone misses it.

---

### D2. The glossary
**Symptom:** invented terms with no context, used as flavour.
**Why it fails:** the reader can't infer them, so they parse as noise.
**Fix:** one new term per scene, always introduced where somebody needs it, always
transparent enough to guess.

---

### D3. The magic that solves the plot
**Symptom:** a device appears in the third act and resolves the central conflict.
**Why it fails:** it retroactively cheapens everything the device did earlier.
**Fix:** magic complicates, moves, or costs. It never resolves.

---

### D4. The single magic system
**Symptom:** one system, used for everything, fully explained.
**Why it fails:** a closed system removes mystery and removes surprise.
**Fix:** at least two, with a gap between them that nobody has explained. The gap is
where the reader's curiosity lives.

---

### D5. The world that exists only for the plot
**Symptom:** you can delete the entire setting and the plot still works.
**Why it fails:** it means the setting was decoration.
**Fix:** change the setting and the plot must change. If it doesn't, build a
setting that does work.

---

## E. Structure-level

### E1. The summary ending
**Symptom:** the last paragraph tells the reader what the character felt about the
chapter.
**Why it fails:** the reader had just worked it out.
**Fix:** end on the turn, or on a line that satisfies.

---

### E2. The chapter that ends on nothing
**Symptom:** the last line is a description of weather.
**Why it fails:** no reason to turn the page.
**Fix:** the turn. Reversal, reveal, decision, or arrival.

---

### E3. Cliffhanger monotony
**Symptom:** every chapter ends on a cliffhanger.
**Why it fails:** the reader learns the pattern, and the *real* cliffhanger lands
with less force.
**Fix:** vary the kind of ending. Not every ending needs to be loud.

---

### E4. The plot-only book
**Symptom:** you could replace the characters with named placeholders and the plot
would work identically.
**Why it fails:** the reader came for the people.
**Fix:** change a character's decision and see if the plot changes. If not, the
plot is driving.

---

### E5. The fortune-teller plot
**Symptom:** the plot can be predicted from the genre.
**Why it fails:** the reader's engagement collapses when they can see the shape.
**Fix:** the corpus's answer — *consequence over prophecy*. The question is never
"who will win" but "who can afford what."

---

### E6. The lecture
**Symptom:** the narrative stops to explain the theme.
**Why it fails:** it hands the reader the conclusion they'd have reached for free.
**Fix:** cut the speech. The theme should be in the *structure* — who wins, who
pays, what nobody says.

---

## F. Romantasy-specific

### F1. The cure
**Symptom:** the love interest heals the heroine's trauma.
**Why it fails:** it reduces a wound to a mechanism.
**Fix:** the romance complicates the wound. The character is not "well" at the end;
they're *different*, and the difference has a cost.

---

### F2. The healing love interest as prop
**Symptom:** he has no arc of his own; he exists to be good to her.
**Why it fails:** the reader sees the scaffolding.
**Fix:** his full six-part engine. His own wound, his own lie, his own
contradiction. And the two contradictions should *interact*.

---

### F3. The tell-show-tell sandwich
**Symptom:** "The room was sunny. The curtains had been pushed back all the way,
which was why. The room was very sunny indeed."
**Why it fails:** it states the fact, dramatises it, then states it again. The
third statement is the writer's hand on the reader's shoulder.
**Fix:** say it once. Choose.

---

### F4. The repetitive evaluative adjective
**Symptom:** the love interest is described as beautiful/hot/etc. in every scene.
**Why it fails:** it's the writer's opinion, repeated until it becomes noise.
**Fix:** describe the *effect* on the POV once, and then let behaviour carry it.

---

### F5. Banter as filler
**Symptom:** the jokes could be exchanged by any two characters in any book.
**Why it fails:** they're not doing character or plot work, so they're a leak.
**Fix:** every joke must attach to something the POV wants. If you can't say what
it attached to, cut the joke.

---

### F6. The world as wallpaper
**Symptom:** the fantasy setting could be swapped for a contemporary one with no
damage.
**Why it fails:** in romantasy the world is half the book. If it doesn't matter, the
book is a romance set in a costume.
**Fix:** the world's problems must be personal to the couple, and the couple's
problems must be made *possible* by the world.

---

### F7. The free pass
**Symptom:** the protagonist is unlikable — cruel, cold, a liar — and the book never
asks them to pay for it.
**Why it fails:** the reader is asked to admire someone whose actions are costless.
**Fix:** cost. In the study, the most likable characters in the corpus are the ones
the book hurts.

---

## G. The meta-antipattern

### G1. The rule-obsessed draft
**Symptom:** the chapter is technically perfect and emotionally inert. Every
sentence obeys. Nothing is at stake.
**Why it fails:** craft in service of nothing. The rules are a means.
**Fix:** write the scene you actually want, badly, first. Then apply the bible as
a *diagnostic*, not a *generator*. The bible tells you what's wrong with the draft
you already have; it should never tell you what to feel.

---

### G2. The pastiche trap
**Symptom:** the chapter reads like *A Song of Ice and Fire* but without Martin's
function — the political machinery, the character depth, the consequence.
**Why it fails:** imitation of surface without mechanism is a costume.
**Fix:** borrow the *mechanism*, not the surface. "Anglo-Saxon vocabulary" is a
mechanism. "Lots of names and a feast" is a costume.

---

## Quick triage

If a chapter is failing, check in this order:

1. **Is the POV making a choice?** (C6)
2. **Is there a turn at the end?** (E1, E2)
3. **Is there consequence?** (Law VI)
4. **Is the dialogue doing two jobs?** (Law IV)
5. **Is the POV's attention specific?** (Law II)
6. **Are the emotion-nouns doing the work that behaviour should?** (A4)
7. **Is anyone asking for the exposition?** (D1)
8. **Is the violence's aftermath somewhere else?** (`09`)
9. **Is the romance complicating the wound?** (F1)
10. **Is the prose enjoying itself?** (A10)

If the answer to most of these is yes and the chapter still fails, the problem is
probably that **nothing is at stake** — and the fix is not in the bible.
