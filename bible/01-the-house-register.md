# 01 · The House Register

**What our prose sounds like, and how it fails.**

---

## 1. The stance

Three words: **plain, exact, warm-underneath-cold.**

- **Plain** — the sentence does not show off. It carries its freight and gets out of
  the way.
- **Exact** — every word means what it says. Not approximately. Exactly.
- **Warm-underneath-cold** — the *surface* of the prose is cool and unsentimental.
  The warmth is in the attention: someone is noticing these particular things about
  this particular person, and that noticing is where the feeling lives.

### The window-pane principle

The best single description of this house's register, and it comes from the
largest-selling fantasy author alive. Brandon Sanderson distinguishes between
**stained-glass prose** — where the prose itself is a pleasure, an ornament
independent of what it shows — and **window-pane prose**, where the prose exists
only to let the reader see through it. He writes toward the window-pane.

**That is our default.** The glass is clean, the pane is thin, and the reader
looks through it at a person.

The glass is allowed to be beautiful. But only where the reader has earned it, and
only for the two or three lines in a chapter that are doing the emotional work.
Everywhere else: clean glass.

Compare:

> ❌ She felt a warm pang of bittersweet longing as she watched him go, her heart
> aching with a wistful ache that seemed to echo through her very soul.

> ✅ He went. She watched him to the door, and did not follow, and was glad she had
> the excuse of the washing.

The first tells us the emotion and gives us no information. The second gives us:
*she wanted to follow; she didn't; she constructed a reason; she is telling herself
it's practical.* The reader feels it. The prose never says "longing."

**The exchange rate:** every abstract emotion word you use costs you a piece of
information you could have given instead. Spend them like money you don't have.

## 2. Diction: the Anglo-Saxon rule

This is the most direct and most imitable lesson from the corpus, and it comes
principally from George R.R. Martin, who — as documented in linguistic analyses of
*A Song of Ice and Fire* — **systematically excludes Latinate, Greek, and
neo-classical vocabulary**, preferring Anglo-Saxon and Norse roots. The effect is
cumulative: a feeling of woodiness, antiquity, and pith. The world sounds old
because its *words* are old.

| Prefer | Over | Why |
|---|---|---|
| work, wage, bread | labour, remuneration, sustenance | Concrete. Physical. |
| stone, iron, wood, hide | material, substrate, medium | You can touch them. |
| hunger, cold, grief, fear | malaise, discomfort, apprehension | Named emotions are more precise than hedged ones. |
| said, asked, told | exclaimed, enquired, murmured | Attribution verbs are not characterisation. |
| broken, cut, burned | damaged, impaired, compromised | Damage has a shape. |
| look, turn, stop, go | observe, cease, proceed | Verbs of the body. |

**Latinate words are for power.** Let them mark it. When a character uses "commence"
rather than "start," they've been educated in a specific way, and that tells us
something the reader absorbs without noticing. **Reserve the elevated register for
elevated people** — then it means something.

### Archaic as seasoning, never as costume

Martin also uses pseudo-medievalisms — "oft," "elsewhere," "amongst," dropped *-ly*
adverbs, and invents compounds ("greybeard," "sevenking"). Linguistic analysis of his
prose finds this layer to be, on its own, a *blemish* on an otherwise clear style:
the effect comes not from the archaisms but from the underlying Germanic vocabulary.
The lesson:

> Archaic diction is salt, not the meal. One in a paragraph is vivid. Three in a
> sentence is a costume, and the reader will hear the costume.

**House rule:** maximum **one** marked archaic construction per page. If you want
more, your sentence is doing the wrong job.

### Banned words

These appear in the corpus's *failures*, not its successes. Cut them on sight:

`breathtaking` · `ethereal` · `ravishing` · `mysterious aura` · `electric` (for
tension) · `heart pounded` / `heart raced` · `eyes widened` · `shivers down spine` ·
`breath caught` · `a shiver ran through` · `couldn't help but` · `in that moment` ·
`little did (she|he) know` · `time seemed to slow` · `a mix of X and Y` ·
`sent a wave of` · `let out a breath (she|he) didn't know (she|he) was holding` ·
`nailed it to the door` · `dead silence` · `the air was thick with tension`

Each of these is a **pre-computed reaction**. The reader has seen it before, so it
carries no information. Replace with what the character actually did.

## 3. The four registers

Every character and every scene sits in one of four registers. Consistency within a
scene is what makes dialogue feel like two people rather than one author.

### Register A — **The Plain**
Short words. Direct statements. No ornament.

> "We go now. Or we don't go, and winter finds us here."
>
> "He's lying."
>
> "Then we're all lying. Get the horses."

**Who speaks like this:** soldiers, farmers, prisoners, the exhausted, the young.
**Where it's used:** the default. Our default.

### Register B — **The Plain with a Blade**
The plain register plus ironic competence. Says the true thing in a way that mocks the
false thing. Not mean — *precise*.

> "You're going to kill him."
>
> "Someone is."
>
> "And you're going to let them."
>
> "Someone lets me. I'm not unreasonable."

**Who speaks like this:** the clever, the senior, the ones who've survived something.
**Where it's used:** the house's signature. Our second default.

### Register C — **The Elevated**
Longer sentences, subordinate clauses, Latinate vocabulary, formal syntax. Says less
per word. Covers what it means.

> "It would appear that our arrangement has been observed by persons whose interest
> in our welfare I confess I am unable to determine."

**Who speaks like this:** the powerful, the educated, the Church, the administration.
**Where it's used:** to *mark* a hierarchy. If everyone spoke Register C, it means
nothing.

### Register D — **The Broken**
Short. Fragmentary. Repetitive. Loses the thread. Returns to one word.

> "She said the second shipment. She said the second. I said it was the first."
>
> "My hands. Not — look at my hands."
>
> "I had it. I had it, I had it, I had it—"

**Who speaks like this:** the traumatised, the starving, the very young, the very
furious.
**Where it's used:** sparingly, and always at a cost to the reader. One broken passage
per book is a shock; three is a tic.

## 4. Tone targets

### Humour
Our humour is **dry, situational, and aimed at institutions rather than people.**

The best evidence in the corpus is Abercrombie: his Glokta is a professional torturer
who finds the world funny because it is funny, and the humour never softens what
happens. It also comes from Pratchett's register — but Pratchett is a *comic* writer
and his fourth-wall jokes are a different instrument. We borrow the *dryness* and
refuse the *jokes about the reader*.

**Rule:** if the joke would be funny if the victim weren't in the room, it is cruel.
If it is funny *because* the victim is in the room and can't do anything about it, it
is comedy of character, and it is allowed.

### Violence
See `09-violence-and-the-body.md`. In brief: **mechanism, consequence, aftermath.**
No glamour. No montage of wounds. The reader should understand the physical
mechanics better than they want to and then feel the cost.

### Sentiment
We may write a gut-punch. We may not announce it. Rule: **one emotional beat per
scene, and it must be carried by behaviour, not by naming the emotion.**

The line "she was devastated" is worth less than "she put the bowl down very
carefully, and then washed it, and then washed it again."

### Restraint
The most powerful thing in the studied corpus is the **omission of the obvious**. When
a character is going to die, the prose does not announce the death. The prose notes
that the character's name hasn't come up in three pages.

## 5. The default failure

Our house style's characteristic failure is **flattering the protagonist**. It comes
from writing the world from the POV's attention and then unconsciously *organising*
that attention around the POV's importance.

Symptoms:
- The POV notices everything important and nothing irrelevant.
- Other characters are described in terms of their function to the POV.
- The POV's mistakes are framed as understandable; others' are framed as failures.
- The POV is never wrong about a person's character on the first read.

**The fix:** give the POV a **specific and limited attention** that has nothing to do
with their importance. Let them notice the horses' hooves. Let them be wrong about
someone. Let them miss the thing that mattered.

## 6. Register ladder — quick test

Score a chapter 0–3 on each axis. If any axis is a 3, you've found the problem.

| Axis | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 — problem |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Ornament** | No wasted words | A few | Frequent | Every sentence |
| **Abstract ratio** | All concrete | Mostly concrete | Frequent emotion-nouns | Emotion-nouns replace evidence |
| **Filter words** | None | A few | Noticeable | "She realised," "He noticed," "seemed to" |
| **Dialogue share** | <35% | 35–50% | 50–65% | <25% (wall of narration) |
| **POV discipline** | Clean | One slip | Two slips | Narrator knows more than POV |
| **Turn at chapter end** | Present | Implied | Weak | Summary or fade |
| **Consequence** | Everyone pays | Most pay | One pays | Nobody pays |
| **Info-dump** | None | One block | Two blocks | Recitation of history/genealogy |

## 7. What this register is *not*

It is not:

- **Stoic.** Stoicism is the absence of feeling. Our characters feel violently and
  badly and are often distracted by it mid-sentence.
- **Grey.** Moral ambiguity is not moral mush. These characters have convictions and
  act on them and are sometimes wrong.
- **Bleak.** The studied corpus is often *warm*. Found family. Grins in the dark.
  Friendship as the thing that survives. Grit is about the world, not about the
  absence of love.
- **Slow.** Slow-burning is a choice about *where* to put the heat, not an excuse for
  a book that doesn't move. The studied corpus runs on momentum.
- **Anti-fantasy.** Low magic doesn't mean no wonder. It means wonder has a price and
  a name.
