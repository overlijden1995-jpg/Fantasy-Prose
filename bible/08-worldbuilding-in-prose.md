# 08 · Worldbuilding in Prose

**Exposition without info-dumps.**

The most common failure in the studied corpus is *not* bad prose. It is the
**info-dump**: a character reciting the history of a nation, or the genealogies of
five houses, to a listener who didn't ask.

---

## 1. The diagnosis

An info-dump has these tells:

1. **Nobody wants the information.** The speaker is explaining because the author
   needs to, not because the character needs to.
2. **The listener doesn't react.** Real people receiving surprising information
   respond. Silence in the face of revelation is a sign the scene is fake.
3. **The information is a paragraph of neutral summary.** No one speaks like that.
4. **It's placed at a moment with no other pressure.** Exposition happens when the
   story stops.
5. **The reader could not possibly need it yet.**

The last one is the most important. If the reader doesn't yet have a framework for
the information, they can't retain it.

---

## 2. The five legitimate channels

### 2.1 Someone wants it
A character needs a rule, a history, a debt, a border drawn — to get something.

> "If she's your sister, then the deed's no good."
>
> "It was witnessed."
>
> "By whom?"
>
> "By a man who's dead, in a town that burned, in a year before the flood."
>
> "Then it was witnessed by nobody at all, and you're an honest woman, so you'll
> say so, and we'll go to the assize like sensible people."

The law of entail, the flood, the burned town — all worldbuilding, all delivered
because two people want the same document and can't both have it.

### 2.2 Someone resents it
The world is shown by what it obliges people to endure.

> "You'll go to Kell," he said. "You'll go to Kell and stand in the rain outside the
> gate and wait until someone comes out, because that's what the Kell does to
> people who come from the valley."
>
> "And if I don't?"
>
> "Then you'll not go to Kell, and you'll go to the fields instead, and the fields
> are worse, but nobody will ask you why."

### 2.3 Someone gets it wrong
The rules are stated by a person with an agenda, so we learn the rule *and* the
agenda at once.

> "The law says a man can't hold a ford in winter," the sergeant said.
>
> "And in summer?"
>
> "In summer he can hold two, and his brother holds the third, and the lord's men
> hold the road." He looked at her. "That's not in the law. That's in the practice.
> The practice is what you'd be standing in."

### 2.4 Someone pays for it
Cost is the fastest route to comprehension.

> The tax on the bridge had been a tenth of the crossing for forty years, and it
> was a fifth now, and the difference between a tenth and a fifth was exactly the
> difference between a cart that made the crossing and a cart that didn't.

### 2.5 Incidental detail
The most underrated channel. Details that nobody is delivering to anyone; the world
simply being the world.

> There were three horses in the yard and two of them were his and one of them was
> somebody's, and the somebody's was the better horse, which was how they knew.

> The market at Ellan had a name for the day the fish came in, and the name was
> not a date but a fish.

## 3. Scale and pace

### 3.1 The information budget

Per chapter, a realistic budget for **new** world facts:

| Type | Per chapter |
|---|---|
| New named people | 2–4 |
| New place names | 1–3 |
| New institutional terms | 1–2 |
| New historical facts | 0–1 |
| New magic rules | 0–1 (and only if the plot needs it now) |

**That's it.** A chapter that introduces eight names is a chapter the reader will
not remember any of them from.

### 3.2 The order of introduction

1. **Introduce the concept in a context where it costs something.**
2. **Show the rule in action before you explain it.**
3. **Explain it only when a character needs to know.**
4. **Show the exception.**

> ❌ "The wardens of the March were bound by the Salt Compact, an agreement made
> in 311 between the seven river lords, which required..."
>
> ✅ "You'll not cross at Lind," she said. "The salt's in the water there and the
> wardens read it."
>
> "They read salt?"
>
> "They read what's in it. Bodies. Boats. Whether the water's been salted." She
> spat. "You want to cross at Lind, cross at the stone bridge and take the long
> road, and if you're carrying anything the March doesn't want carried, you'll
> want to take it over the ford at Ashlett instead, and don't ask me why."

Nobody needed a history of the Salt Compact. Somebody needed to know where to cross
a river. The reader now has: a March, wardens, salt, reading, bodies in water, a
stone bridge, a long road, and a ford at Ashlett. All from four lines.

### 3.3 Introduce characters before they're POVs

Robert Jordan's structural technique, and it's a good one: **an existing POV learns
something about a character before that character takes a POV.** The reader therefore
arrives at the new POV already knowing something about them, and the new POV's
chapter is not a cold open.

It also solves the "six unrelated plotlines" problem: the new POV's thread *branches
from* an existing one, so the reader tracks it as a branch, not a stranger.

## 4. Making the map

### 4.1 The three-layer world

Every world in the corpus has at least three layers, and the layers must be in
tension:

| Layer | Question | Example shape |
|---|---|---|
| **The ground** | What is physically here? | Cold, wet, poor, far from anything |
| **The institution** | Who is organised and how? | The March; the guild; the crown; the Church |
| **The belief** | What do people think is true? | That the wardens read salt; that the gods listen |

The **belief layer** is the one that gets skipped, and it's the one that makes a
world feel real. Beliefs are always *partly wrong*, and the story is usually the
moment the wrongness becomes expensive.

### 4.2 The economy

The corpus's realism comes largely from **economic specificity**:

- What does a thing cost, in what currency, and who can afford it?
- Who pays the tax? Who collects it? Who eats because of it?
- What's the labour? What's the season? What does the season do to the plot?

> "How much for the passage?"
>
> "The going rate's four."
>
> "It's the wrong season."
>
> "Then it's five."
>
> "Six, then, and you can have the cabin, and I'll not tell the captain."
>
> "Six, and I'll not tell him the cabin's got a hole in it."

The economy of a novel is not decoration; it's the plot's constraint.

### 4.3 The calendar

The studied corpus uses **season, weather, and distance** as the primary pacing
instruments. A journey is measured in days, food, and light. A siege is measured in
weeks and what the besiegers can eat.

> "How long?"
>
> "By the road, nine days. By the coast, twelve, and if the weather's in it,
> twenty."
>
> "So nine."
>
> "So nine if we leave now, and if we leave now the ford's still passable, and if
> we leave in three days the ford's a mile of mud and we go the long way, and the
> long way is past Kell."

One exchange: the season, the ford, the weather, Kell, the river. All delivered as a
bargain over a price.

## 5. Magic

### 5.1 The rules of good magic in this house

1. **Magic is a resource with a price and a supply.** It is made. It runs out. It
   is stored. It is transported. It has a *cost in bodies, labour, or time*.
2. **Magic is socially distributed.** Some people have it, some can't, some have
   bought it, some are ruined by it. The social structure of magic is more
   interesting than the magic.
3. **Magic never solves the plot.** It complicates it, or it moves the problem.
4. **The cost is paid by someone, and the reader can see who.**
5. **Limits are visible in the prose.** Not in a rule list — in the characters'
   *habits around it*. A character who can only do a thing at night, or only once a
   year, or only with another's help, shows us the limit by working around it.

### 5.2 The low-magic discipline

In this house, magic is **rare, costly, and mostly off-stage**. The studied
corpus's low-magic books get their power from the *absence* of power: the character
who can't do the thing, the world that can't be fixed.

> The best magic beat in the study is one where the cost is unambiguous and paid
> immediately by a named person.

### 5.3 Naming magic

> If the magic has rules, the *language* should reflect them: if a spell takes
> blood, the words for it should be blood-words. If it costs memory, the words
> should cost something to say. And if the magic is not real — if it's faith, or
> superstition, or the survivors' story of what happened — then the words should
> sound like *that*.

## 6. The world and the POV's attention

The filter rule from `05` applies to worldbuilding too. **Different POVs should
notice different aspects of the same place**, and the difference should be character.

> **A soldier** in the market notices the number of people and where they're
> standing.
>
> **A priest** notices the shrine, and whether it's been swept.
>
> **A thief** notices the exits, the height of the eaves, and who's watching.
>
> **A widow** notices the price of bread and whether the baker has raised it.

The same market, four chapters, four paragraphs — and now the reader has four
characters and a city, without a single line of description for its own sake.

---

## 7. The exposition self-check

For each block of worldbuilding in the chapter:

1. **Who wants it?** Name the person.
2. **What do they get?** What changes if they get it?
3. **Does the listener react?** If not, add the reaction.
4. **Is it spoken in a character's voice,** or is it narration?
5. **Is it needed *now*?** Would the reader have a framework to retain it?
6. **Could it be cut?** If the reader would not miss it, cut it.
7. **Does it cost anything?** If the information is free, it's not story.

**The red-flag phrase:** *the history of X dates back to Y*, *it was well known
that*, *as every scholar knows*, *little did he know*. All of these mean the author
needed it.
