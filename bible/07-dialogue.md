# 07 · Dialogue

**The primary instrument.**

The most reliable statistic from the study: in the strongest serial fantasy in the
corpus, **roughly half to two-thirds of the words on the page are dialogue.** The
narration exists to get us into the room, to hold what can't be said aloud, and to
land the turn.

---

## 1. The five jobs

Every line of dialogue must do at least **two** of these. A line that does one is
filler.

| Job | Question | Failure if absent |
|---|---|---|
| **Advance** | Does this change who knows what, or what the situation is? | The conversation is a detour |
| **Characterise** | Does this reveal *this* speaker — their diction, evasion, education, class? | It's interchangeable |
| **Subtext** | Is the real content in what isn't said? | It's exposition wearing a costume |
| **Pressure** | Does this force the other party to reveal, concede, or evade? | It's two people taking turns |
| **Texture** | Does this establish register, region, profession, relationship history? | The world is mute |

**The signature test:** remove the speaker tags. Could a different character have
said this line? If yes, it isn't finished.

---

## 2. Subtext: the gap is the point

Subtext isn't about being coy. It's about **two things being true at once** — one
said, one meant.

### 2.1 The three classic techniques

**Oblivious subtext** — the speaker says something enormous without noticing its
weight.

> "You're leaving, then."
>
> "In the morning."
>
> "Right." He looked at the fire. "The roads will be mud by then. Past the weir it's
> been mud since the feast."
>
> "Then I'll go early."
>
> "You'll go early," she agreed, and neither of them said anything else, and the
> fire went down and down.

**Misdirected subtext** — both characters know the real subject and talk around it.

> "The west road's open again."
>
> "Is it."
>
> "Two days' ride. There's a bridge at Marren now."
>
> "There wasn't one at Marren before."
>
> "There's one at Marren now," he said, and she looked at him for a moment too long,
> and then they both looked at the fire.

**Status subtext** — the same sentence means different things to different people
because of their positions.

> "Please sit."
>
> Two words. One of them was a courtesy and the other was an instruction, and which
> one it was depended entirely on who had said them and how loudly.

### 2.2 The rule

> **Subtext by omission, not by euphemism.** The studied corpus's realist mode does
> not have characters speaking in code. They speak plainly, and the *plainness* is
> what makes the subtext visible. (The exception is characters who are professionally
> oblique — a torturer, a diplomat, a spymaster — and for them the obliqueness is
> characterisation, which is a different mechanism.)

## 3. Distinct voices

### 3.1 Voice is a system, not an accent

Abercrombie's craft, which is the corpus's most transferable lesson in dialogue:
**each POV character has a different tone of voice, and the *narration* is written in
it too.** Not just the speech — the prose itself shifts register per character.

This is why you can tell which character you're in from the first paragraph with no
identifying information. It's the same principle as the filter rule in `05`, applied
to language.

### 3.2 The voice components

For each speaking character, fix:

| Component | Options |
|---|---|
| **Vocabulary** | Plain / elevated / technical / cant |
| **Contractions** | Heavy / moderate / none (a formal signaller) |
| **Sentence length** | Short and clipped / medium / long and winding |
| **Humour** | Deadpan / broad / absent / weaponised |
| **Formality** | Ranked by deference to the listener |
| **Evasion** | Direct / oblique / answers a different question |
| **Punctuation habit** | Trailing off / hard stops / questions / fragments |
| **What they never say** | The most revealing part |

### 3.3 The "what they never say" test

Every character has a subject they don't discuss. A soldier doesn't discuss what he
felt. A courtesan doesn't discuss her mother. A king doesn't discuss his age.

**This is the cheapest and most efficient way to differentiate voices**, and it
doubles as characterisation. Give each major character one taboo subject, and let
the dialogue deflect from it whenever it approaches.

### 3.4 Dialogue tags

The house rule:

- **Never tag with an emotion.** `said sadly` is a failure — the line was sad enough.
- **Vary the tag.** If every tag is "said," use some of: *asked, replied, said,
  said, went on, said, said* — with **action beats** interspersed, which are the
  superior option.
- **Action beats, not emotions.** `She closed the ledger.` Not `she said grimly.`
- **The single-word tag is the strongest.** `"No." Malik said.` carries more
  than `"No," he said sadly.`

**The dialogue-beat as characterisation:**
> "I don't know," she said. The knife went back in the sheath.
>
> "Then why are you here?"
>
> "I'm not," she said, and went through the door.

> **Never tag with a bodily reaction the character can't control** (a flush, a
> stammer) unless the flush *is* the point — and then, once, not every time.

## 4. Dialogue that carries plot

### 4.1 The bargain

The most efficient scene in serial fantasy: two people want incompatible things and
talk it through, and the reader watches the terms move. Almost nothing happens
physically. Everything happens.

> "I want the ledger," she said.
>
> "You want the ledger."
>
> "I want the ledger, and I want you to stop asking me why."
>
> "That's two wants."
>
> "It's one want and a condition."
>
> He poured the second cup and did not drink it. "You know what the second cup's
> for."
>
> "I know what the second cup's for. I want the ledger."
>
> "Then drink it."
>
> "Then you'll give me the ledger."
>
> "Then you'll drink it." He turned the cup a quarter-turn on the table, which was
> the only concession he had ever made to anything, and she looked at it, and then at
> him, and then she drank it, and it was not good, and she drank all of it.

Every line does two jobs. The negotiation *is* the plot. The turn is at the end.

### 4.2 The interrogation

Two people, one with power, one without, and the power is in the questions. This is
Abercrombie's specialty and it generalises:

- The questioner states a **falsehood** they know to be false, to see if the
  subject corrects it.
- The subject answers a **different question** than the one asked.
- Silence is used as a tool.
- The questioner **stops asking** the thing they actually want, and waits.

### 4.3 The interruption

Characters get interrupted, lose their train, contradict themselves, and start over.
Real conversation is not well-made.

> "The thing about the—" he stopped. "Sorry. Go on."
>
> "About the ledger?"
>
> "About the bridge. No. The ledger. Yes."

This is free characterisation and free texture. Use it once per scene at most.

## 5. Interiority and dialogue

### 5.1 The classic problem

In close third, you can't narrate what the POV is thinking *during* a conversation
without breaking the scene. The standard solutions:

| Solution | Risk |
|---|---|
| **Filter the dialogue through the POV's attention** — what they notice, what they're reading in it | Can become overwrought if overused |
| **Interleave short internal beats** between lines | Fragments the scene |
| **Render the reaction physically, in the other character's body** | Only works if POV is external |
| **Use FID to let the POV's judgement intrude** | Best option; see `02-syntax.md` §5 |

### 5.2 The judgement filter — the house's best tool

Instead of narrating what the POV thinks, let their judgement colour what they
observe in the other's words:

> "I'm sure it's fine," he said, in the voice he used when he was counting.
>
> **Not fine.** She'd heard that voice for nine years and it had never once been
> fine. She looked at the letter again and did not read it again, because reading it
> again would make it true, and it was not true yet, and she was not going to be the
> one who made it true.

The narration knows what the character believes and lets the belief *do* work —
reordering the character's actions around it. That's not a filter word; that's a
rendered mind.

## 6. Violence in dialogue

The studied corpus's approach, and it is worth copying exactly:

**The body speaks before the mouth does.**

> He was already sitting down when she came in, which was wrong, and she'd known him
> nineteen years, and in nineteen years he had never once been in a room before she
> was.
>
> "You're early," she said.
>
> "You're late."

> There's no discussion. The line has already been drawn by the fact that he was
> sitting down.

**Rules:**
1. Never explain the violence in dialogue afterwards. The characters don't
   narrate their own injuries to each other.
2. Let the *other* character notice the evidence. The limp. The shirt sleeve.
   The way he puts weight wrong.
3. Characters who are used to violence talk *around* it. That's the register.

## 7. Registers of dialogue

| Register | Who | Shape |
|---|---|---|
| **Plain** | Soldiers, farmers, the exhausted | Short. Flat. No subtext. |
| **Plain with a blade** | The clever, the senior, the survivors | Says the true thing in a way that mocks the false one |
| **Elevated** | The powerful, the Church, the administration | Long sentences, subordinate clauses, says less per word |
| **Broken** | The traumatised, the young, the furious | Fragmentary. Repetitive. Returns to one word |
| **Operational** | Spies, soldiers on the move, crews | Clipped. Codes. Confirmations. No wasted syllable |
| **Romantic** | Two people who are circling | Long sentences with shared clauses, self-interruption, half-finished lines |

**A rule for the romantic register:** the sentence must contain both people at once.
"Hand me that" said to a person who was already handing it to you. That's the
grammar of intimacy. (See `02-syntax.md` §7.)

## 8. The "she said / he said" problem

If your dialogue is 80% "said," the scene is under-invented. Two fixes:

1. **Action beats.** Not "she said" but "she said, and pushed the book toward him."
   Two jobs in one line.
2. **Silence.** The most under-used instrument in the corpus.

> "You didn't tell me."
>
> He looked at the door.
>
> "You didn't tell me," she said again, and it was not a question the second time.

> "I know what you're going to say."
>
> "Then say it."
>
> "No."
>
> "That's not the same as saying it."
>
> "No," he agreed. "It isn't."

---

## 9. Dialogue self-check

1. **Two jobs** — for each line, name two.
2. **Interchangeability** — remove the tag. Could someone else say it?
3. **The taboo** — is this character avoiding their subject?
4. **Register match** — is the dialogue in the right register for this character and
   this moment?
5. **Tag discipline** — any emotion tags? Any "he said, his voice soft with X"?
6. **Silence** — is there a place where nobody speaks and it means something?
7. **Pressure** — does each exchange force something?
8. **Ratio** — is dialogue above 50% of the scene?
9. **The first line** — could the scene open with it, mid-argument?
