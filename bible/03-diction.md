# 03 · Diction

**Choosing words, and building a world's vocabulary.**

---

## 1. The specificity ladder

When you reach for a word, climb this ladder and stop at the first rung that carries
the meaning.

| Rung | Example | What it costs you |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Abstract | "beautiful" | Everything. The reader learns nothing. |
| 2. Categorical | "pretty", "handsome", "striking" | The specific *kind*. |
| 3. Attributive | "a strikingly handsome man" | Same as above, with a badge of effort. |
| 4. Comparativist | "handsome the way a well-kept tool is handsome" | A simile, and a borrowed comparison. |
| 5. **Material** | "handsome in the way the inside of her wrist was, where the vein stood up under the skin" | Nothing. This is free specificity. |

**The rule:** rung 5 is free; rungs 1–4 are a tax on the reader's patience. Almost
every adjective is on rung 2 or 3.

**Test:** for each adjective in the chapter, ask *what would I see if I looked?* If
the answer isn't a physical detail, it's a rung-2 adjective and it should go.

---

## 2. Concrete vs. abstract nouns

Nouns are where sentences get heavy. Prefer the object to the category.

> ❌ "The situation was precarious."
> ✅ "The rope had frayed through a third of its width and the wind had not stopped
> since noon."
>
> ❌ "There was tension in the room."
> ✅ "Nobody sat down. Three of them had found reasons to stand near the door."

**The test:** put the noun next to a number, a colour, a material, or a measurement.
If it can't take one, it's an abstraction, and abstractions are usually a way of
avoiding a scene.

**Exception:** abstract nouns are exactly right for *interior states that resist
physical description* — and the way to use them well is to give them a container:
"an old tiredness," "a professional dislike," "the specific embarrassment of a person
who has been right about the weather." That last construction — **abstract noun
qualified by a circumstance** — is one of the house's favourite moves. It makes the
feeling particular to this person and this moment.

## 3. Verbs

Verbs carry the sentence. Choose them last, and choose them for accuracy over
elegance.

### 3.1 Specific verbs

> ❌ "She moved toward the door."
> ✅ "She edged toward the door, keeping her shoulder to him."

Same information; the second tells you the fear, the tactics, and the geometry of
the room. And it does it without a single emotion word.

### 3.2 Verbs of the body for interiority

When you need to render a feeling, find a verb that belongs to the body.

| Feeling | Flat | Specific |
|---|---|---|
| Fear | "She was afraid." | "She kept her back to the wall and her eyes on the door." |
| Anger | "He was furious." | "He put the cup down harder than he meant to and did not pick it up again." |
| Shame | "She was embarrassed." | "She kept her hands in her lap, palms up, like someone waiting to be searched." |
| Relief | "She felt relieved." | "She sat down. She hadn't realised she was still standing." |
| Grief | "He was devastated." | "He said her name once, in the way you say a word you've said too many times to mean, and then he didn't say it again." |

**The house rule:** no emotion is rendered by an emotion-noun *or* an emotion-verb
("frowned," "smiled sadly," "snarled"). It's rendered by a **choice** the character
makes and the **cost** of that choice.

### 3.3 Avoid the shading verbs

`smiled`, `frowned`, `sighed`, `shrugged`, `laughed`, `shook his head` — these are
fine in moderation. Used as characterisation they're inert; used as the *only*
response in a scene they're a tic.

Replace with the specific action:
> ❌ "He shrugged." → ✅ "He put one hand up, palm out, and took a step back."

## 4. Building a world's lexicon

A fantasy world's vocabulary is a **system**, not a decoration. The studied corpus
that does this best is *Wheel of Time*, where culture is delivered through *how
people curse*. Cultures differ by what they consider unspeakable, and the profanity
of a people is more informative than a paragraph describing their temples.

### The rules

1. **Every culture gets one word it uses for things others use several.** And vice
   versa. If the northern captain says "murder" and the court says "execution," you
   have built two cultures in two words.
2. **Register is inherited.** A word's *form* tells you who uses it. Contractions,
   archaisms, technical jargon, trade terms.
3. **Names carry their own etymology.** Build a phonology and stick to it. If one
   culture's names are monosyllabic and hard-consonanted and another's are soft and
   three-syllabled, the reader will feel the difference before understanding it.
4. **Introduce jargon at the moment of need, in the mouth of someone who needs it.**
   Never in narration; never in an aside; never in a glossary the reader has to
   consult.
5. **Specialised vocabulary for specialists.** A farrier's words, a ship's words, a
   herbalist's words, a clerk's words. This is the cheapest possible worldbuilding
   and it's the most convincing, because people who know things talk like people who
   know things.

### 4.1 The conquest test

A word enters a culture by **conquest, trade, or craft** — and you can show all three
in a single scene:

> The garrison had been English for forty years, but the word for the market square
> was still *the green*, and nobody could say where that had come from, and nobody
> would say the English word for it out loud either.

One sentence. Two cultures, forty years of contact, a power imbalance, a stubborn
local identity, and a refusal to assimilate — delivered as *a detail about a place*.

### 4.2 Archaic and invented words

The studied corpus invents words freely. Rules from the failures:

- **Invent compounds, not roots.** Martin's compounds ("greybeard," "sevenking") are
  Anglo-Saxon-transparent: you can guess them. A root you can't guess ("nakt" for
  "night") requires a footnote or a dictionary, and the reader will not do either
  mid-scene.
- **One invented term per scene, maximum.** A scene with three unfamiliar coined
  words reads as a tax on the reader.
- **Never coin the word for the thing the plot turns on.** The reader must be able to
  say "oh — *that* word" without stopping.

## 5. Numbers, quantities, and the concrete

Quantities are the cheapest realism in prose.

> ❌ "There were many soldiers."
> ✅ "Forty. Maybe fifty. Enough that nobody was going to try to count them."

> ❌ "She waited a long time."
> ✅ "She waited until the water stopped moving and then a little longer, because
> the first time she'd trusted it, it had started again."

Precision about *how much* and *how long* is what makes a world feel measured rather
than imagined. **Use numbers. Use them oddly.** "Forty" is stranger and more
believable than "dozens."

---

## 6. The title problem

Titles of nobles, offices, and institutions are where fantasy prose most often
becomes costume.

**Rule:** every title must be **load-bearing** — it must tell us something about the
person's power, their relationship to the crown, their duties, or their vulnerability.
If the title could be swapped for any other title without changing the sentence's
meaning, it's wallpaper.

> ❌ "Ser Alaric of House Vane, the Knight of the Silver Watch"
> ✅ "Ser Alaric, who had been the crown's man in the west for eleven years and had
> stopped pretending to be liked for most of them"

The second is a title expressed as *a position and a reputation*, which is both more
specific and more characterising. **Prefer the job to the honorific.**

## 7. Register drift — the house's stealthiest failure

Register drift happens when the writer's mood leaks into the prose mid-scene. A
chapter written flat and controlled will suddenly, in a paragraph of heightened
feeling, become lyrical. The reader feels the seam.

**Diagnostic:** find any sentence in your chapter where the prose is more beautiful
than it needs to be. Ask: *is that because of the character, or because of me?*

**The fix:** rewrite that sentence at the register the scene has been running. Save
the beauty for the one or two places in the book where the reader has earned it.

---

## 8. Diction by POV

The most direct lesson from Abercrombie's craft: **each POV character carries a
different vocabulary, and the narration is written in it.** Not an accent — a
*lexicon*.

- The northern captain's narration uses short, earthy, physical words: *rain, iron,
  cold, meat, blood*.
- The torturer's narration is precise, clinical, and fond of the vocabulary of his
  trade — he notices things the way a craftsman notices things.
- The noble's narration is formal and a little stiff, and he doesn't have the words
  for what he feels, so he reaches for borrowed ones.

**House test:** take any two POV chapters and swap their narratives. If the swap
doesn't feel wrong, the characters don't have different attentions yet. Fix the
character, not the prose.

---

## 9. Self-check

1. **Adjective audit** — every adjective: what would I *see*? Replace or cut.
2. **Abstract noun audit** — can this noun take a number or a material?
3. **Verb audit** — is the verb doing a job a more precise verb could do?
4. **Emotion-noun audit** — every feeling-word. Is there a behaviour nearby that
   could replace it?
5. **Register audit** — find the most beautiful sentence in the chapter. Is it
   earned?
6. **Lexicon audit** — pick any two characters. Would they use different words for
   the same object?
7. **Conquest test** — could a reader learn something about this world's history
   from a single word?
