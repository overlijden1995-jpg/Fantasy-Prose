# 04 · Rhythm & Pacing

**Scene shape, chapter shape, and the turn.**

---

## 1. The unit of momentum is the scene, not the chapter

Most writers think in chapters. Readers think in scenes. Serial fiction readers think
in **chapter ends**, because that's where they decide whether to keep going.

Three nested rhythms:

| Level | Typical length | What controls it |
|---|---|---|
| **Beat** | 1–3 sentences | A change in information, intention, or feeling |
| **Scene** | 800–2,500 words | A value shift: the character wants X, ends wanting not-X |
| **Chapter** | 2,500–5,000 words | A turn that re-frames what came before |

**The scene is the thing that must work.** A chapter that contains two good scenes
and one bad one reads as two good scenes and one bad one. A chapter that is one
scene stretched over 4,000 words reads as slow regardless of how good the prose is.

---

## 2. The scene value-shift

Every scene, at minimum:

```
[State A]  ──pressure──▶  [State B]
```

where A and B are **different in a way that costs something**. Not "the character
learned a fact" (that's exposition). "The character wanted X and now wants Y, and
getting Y will cost them Z."

**Test:** write the scene's first line and last line side by side. If they could
interchange with any other scene's, the scene has no shape.

Examples of a real value shift:

> **A:** Vorn wants the ledger destroyed before the shipment arrives.
> **B:** Vorn has burned it, and now has to tell his father he did it, and the
> shipment still arrives.

> **A:** The girl wants to be left alone in the dark room.
> **B:** She has been left alone in the dark room, and she has decided to go out into
> the hall, and she has no idea what she'll do when she gets there.

## 3. Scene openings

Three reliable openings, in order of preference for this house:

### 3.1 Open in motion, mid-sentence
The scene is already happening. The reader arrives late and works out what's going on.

> He was two hours into the ride and she had stopped pretending to sleep.

Fast, disorienting, confident. Very effective for serial fiction.

### 3.2 Open on a line that costs something
A fact with weight, delivered flat.

> The third shipment was late.

> She had been told the boy was dead.

### 3.3 Open on an action with a consequence
Someone does something that cannot be undone.

> She signed it.

**Avoid:** weather openings, dream openings, "it was a dark and stormy night"
openings, and openings where a character wakes up and thinks about their life. All
four announce that nothing is happening yet.

### The exception
Chapter openings may begin in a quieter register than scene openings, because a
chapter can afford to settle. But **the first page** must still have a turn in it.

---

## 4. Chapter endings: the turn

This is the single highest-leverage habit in the corpus.

**A chapter ends on a turn**, and the turn is one of four kinds:

| Turn | What it does | Example shape |
|---|---|---|
| **Reversal** | The reader re-reads what just happened differently | "The man she'd saved had been the one who'd sold them." |
| **Reveal** | New information, not previously available | "The second shipment had never been a shipment." |
| **Decision** | The character commits, and can't uncommit | "She went down the stairs." |
| **Arrival** | Something arrives that changes everything | A door opening. A name spoken. |

**Never end on:**
- A summary of the chapter's feelings ("And so she slept, uneasy, wondering what
  would come next.")
- A fade-out ("The night passed. Morning came.")
- A rhetorical question ("Would she find him before it was too late?")
- A restatement of the last line of dialogue

### 4.1 The act break

The technique comes straight from screenwriting, where Martin's background taught
him to end each chapter "on a tense or revelational moment, a twist or a cliffhanger
— a TV act break." It's not a gimmick; it's the correct unit of suspense.

**But there's a trap:** if every chapter ends on a cliffhanger, the reader learns to
expect it, and then the *last* chapter's cliffhanger lands with less force.

**House rule:** alternate. Some chapters end on a turn, some on a decision, some on a
scene that simply closes well. Variety in the *kind* of ending preserves the force of
each individual ending.

### 4.2 The false turn

The most advanced version: end the chapter on something that *seems* to be the turn,
is the turn, and then the next chapter reveals there's a bigger turn behind it.

> **Chapter ends:** "So the debt was settled."
> **Next chapter opens:** Three pages later we learn the debt was not settled, that
> the person who said so was lying, and that the lie was for our benefit.

This works because the reader's closure is revoked rather than their information
being extended.

## 5. Pacing within a scene

### 5.1 Compression points

Certain material **must** be compressed or cut:
- Travel that changes nothing
- Introductions between people who already know each other (Weeks' own stated rule:
  "they made their introductions")
- Preparations the reader can imagine
- Explanation of something the POV could discover

**Weeks' rule, worth stealing:** when writing a conversation, skip the boring parts.
If the readers know all of them and they don't know each other, write "they made
their introductions."

### 5.2 Expansion points

Certain material **must** be expanded:
- The moment before the turn
- The moment a character realises something they don't want to know
- The first time we see a person we will see often
- The detail that will matter later (planted plainly, so it can be paid off)

### 5.3 The ratio

A rough but useful target for this house:

> **70% present-tense action and dialogue / 30% interiority and reflection**

The studied corpus sits in this range. The failure modes are:
- **Too much interiority** — the book becomes a diary. (Hobb's *Liveship Traders* is
  the cautionary example among the studied works; her *Farseer* books are the
  successful ones, because they're *first-person*, so the interiority *is* the scene.)
- **Too little** — the book becomes a plot summary. (Fonda Lee's second volume, *Jade
  War*, is frequently criticised for exactly this: summarising conversations and
  stating feelings rather than dramatising them.)

---

## 6. The opening pages of a book

The studied corpus has a strong, consistent pattern in first chapters:

1. **Establish the POV and their competence or their lack of it, quickly.**
2. **Put them in a situation with a cost.**
3. **Refuse to explain the world** — assume the reader can keep up.
4. **Give the POV a want before the end of the first chapter.**

And a warning from *Malazan*, which is a useful negative example: *Gardens of the
Moon* drops the reader into a conflict with no orientation, and many readers never
recover. The genre's "start in the middle" trick works only if the reader is given
enough to *hold*. Brown's *Malazan* demonstrates the depth possible in this mode;
it also demonstrates the accessibility cost.

**House rule:** we may withhold *information*. We may never withhold *orientation*.
The reader should always know where they are, roughly when, and who they're with.
Withhold *why*. Never *where*.

---

## 7. Serialisation rhythm

For books written to be read in instalments:

- **Chapter length is uniform.** Readers plan their sessions. A 1,500-word chapter
  and a 9,000-word chapter in the same book reads as a broken book.
- **Each chapter must be independently rewarding**, even if it advances nothing.
  It should give the reader a moment, an image, a line they want to repeat, or a
  small revelation.
- **The turn is the contract.** The reader who trusts the contract will keep buying.
  Break it once and you lose them for the series.

---

## 8. The reading-aloud test for pacing

From Le Guin's own method: read it aloud and listen for where the story crawls or
races.

**Three diagnostic failures:**

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| The reader keeps checking their phone | No turn inside 1,500 words | Move the turn earlier, or add one |
| The reader skims | Description blocking action | Cut the third detail about anything |
| The reader stops before the chapter ends | No turn | Move the turn to the last line |

**And the one that catches people:** the reader who reads the last three pages at
double speed. That's usually a good sign — that's the turn pulling them forward.

---

## 9. Self-check

1. **Scene shape** — first line vs. last line. Different in a way that costs?
2. **Opening** — in motion, costly, or irreversible? Or is nothing happening yet?
3. **Turn** — is there a turn in the last five paragraphs? What kind?
4. **Endings variety** — across the last five chapters, are the turns different kinds?
5. **Ratio** — 70/30?
6. **Compression** — any travel, introduction, or preparation that could go?
7. **Aloud** — where does it crawl?
