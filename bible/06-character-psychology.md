# 06 · Character Psychology

**How people are actually built on the page.**

This is the core of the study. Across twenty series and ~30 million words, the
psychological machinery of the best adult fantasy is remarkably consistent — and
remarkably different from what most writing advice says.

---

## 1. The thesis

> **A character is not a set of traits. A character is a set of *contradictions
> with a history*.**

Everything else in this chapter is a consequence of that.

Traits are what a character *is*. Contradictions are what a character *does*, and
they are what makes the reader care. Readers do not fall in love with consistent
people. They fall in love with people who are trying to be something they cannot
quite manage, and who fail at it in ways they didn't choose.

---

## 2. The six-part engine

For every character who holds a POV, fill this in. If you can't fill all six, they
don't need a POV.

### 1. WANT — the conscious goal
Specific, actionable, and *not the same as the story's goal for them*. The character
wants something that is good for them and bad for the plot. This is the engine of
comic and tragic irony: we watch them pursue their want and recognise, before they
do, that it will cost them what they actually need.

> *Jade City*: Hilo wants his clan's honour restored. Lan wants the family to
> survive intact. Both are reasonable. Both conflict with the third thing —
> Shae, who wants a life of her own.

### 2. NEED — the unconscious requirement
What they must change to be whole. The reader can feel this before the character
can. The gap between want and need is the arc.

### 3. WOUND — the specific past event
Not "tragic backstory." A **specific event**, with a **date**, in a **place**, that
did a **particular thing** to them. The more specific, the more it constrains the
present.

Bad: "She had a tragic childhood."
Good: "She was nine, and she had been left in a barn with the door barred, for
eleven hours, and she had counted the flies."

The specificity is what allows the wound to *behave* on the page — to fire at
predictable moments, in response to predictable stimuli.

### 4. LIE — the false belief they operate on
The most powerful single element, and the one most often missing.

> **Lie:** "If I don't need anyone, I won't be left."
> **Want:** Control of the situation.
> **Need:** To be able to depend on someone.
> **Behaviour:** Refuses help. Tests people. Leaves before they can leave her.
> **Crack:** Someone leaves first — not out of cruelty, but out of their own damage —
> and she discovers the fear was the cause and not the cure.

The Lie is what makes a character *psychological* rather than *plot-shaped*. It
explains why they make choices that hurt them.

### 5. FEAR — the specific dread
Not "fear of failure." A sensory, particular thing.

> "She was afraid of being laughed at. Not the big laugh, the room one. The small one
> that two people make at the same time and don't both look up."

### 6. CONTRADICTION — the engine of the engine
The pair of truths that cannot both be true, and which the character holds anyway.

> "She was a good doctor and she did not want to be one."
> "He was loyal absolutely and he had already betrayed them twice."
> "He was the best man in the company and he had been keeping a list."

**The contradiction is the character.** You can delete a character's name and
replace them with a word — *capable*, *cruel*, *generous*, *loyal* — and the
character will be lost. But a contradiction is unique. Two characters cannot both
be "a good doctor who did not want to be one" and be the same character.

**House rule:** every POV character must have a contradiction that would be
contradictory in a stranger.

## 3. How psychology is *rendered* (not described)

The studied corpus's most important craft instruction is this:

> **Never tell the reader what a character feels. Render the feeling's evidence.**

But "render the evidence" is too vague to be useful. Here are the seven specific
mechanisms, in ascending order of subtlety:

### 3.1 Behaviour substitution
The character does the emotional thing instead of the practical thing.

> She's grieving. She doesn't think about grief — she *reorganises the shelf*,
> because reorganising is what she does when she can't think, and the jar of
> buttons goes on the wrong hook and she leaves it there for a week.

### 3.2 The wrong register
The character's *register* shifts under stress, in a way only they would.

> The man was a slow speaker. He was also, when frightened, a fast one, and nobody
> in the room had ever heard him do it before, and that was how they knew.

### 3.3 The involuntary cue
A stimulus triggers a physical response the character didn't consent to.

> The smell of the smokehouse. She had not been in one since she was seven. Her
> hands went on doing what they were doing, which was the thing she had been
> watching them do, which was nothing much.

### 3.4 Compulsion and displacement
The character substitutes a small behaviour for a large feeling.

> He checked the bolt on the door. Then he checked it again. He had done this
> every night since the second winter, and he had never once admitted to it,
> including to himself, and he was not going to start.

### 3.5 The lie told publicly
The character tells a version of their past that has become load-bearing, and we
watch it work on other people.

### 3.6 The gap in the memory
The character misremembers, and the prose reports the memory as fact.

> Hobb's *Farseer* books are narrated by an older Fitz. Because the older Fitz has
> since lived through things the younger Fitz hadn't, his recollection of early
> events is systematically *less* reliable than his recollection of recent ones. The
> reader feels the discrepancy without ever being told about it. This is the single
> most elegant psychological device in the studied corpus.

### 3.7 The reader knows more than the character
The most powerful: we watch the character build a false model of someone, and we
watch them *act on it*.

## 4. Sympathy for the unlikeable

The studied corpus's signature achievement is making us care about people we should
not: a torturer (Glokta), a rapist and thief (Locke), a slave-turned-pirate
(Kennit), an abusive king, a man who has done atrocities (Kellhus). This is the
central technical problem of gritty fantasy and it has a known solution.

### 4.1 The four-step method

1. **Give them a real competence.** Not admirable — *real*. Glokta is genuinely
   excellent at his job. The prose renders his expertise with the same precision
   the prose renders a surgeon's. Competence is fascinating and it earns the
   reader's attention before judgement can intervene.

2. **Give them a body that costs them.** Pain that is present in every scene.
   Abercrombie's Glokta thinks in terms of stairs, of the ache in his back, of how
   far he can walk. The prose makes his disability *structural* rather than
   sentimental — it shapes every sentence he can say.

3. **Give them a reason the reader can accept, even if not forgive.** Not a
   justification — a *reason*. "This is what was done to me and this is the only
   thing I know how to do with it." The reader's sympathy is a byproduct of
   comprehension, not of excuse.

4. **Give them one thing they do right, consistently, at cost.** One thing. For
   Glokta it's his refusal to pretend. For Locke it's the crew. For Kennit it's a
   capacity for love he keeps failing to act on.

### 4.2 The trap: the charming sociopath

The most common failure. A character who is *entertaining* and *cruel* reads as
a puzzle to be enjoyed rather than a person to be judged, and the book becomes
clever instead of true.

**The fix:** make the cruelty *cost them something*. Make them unable to do the
kind thing without a physical or social cost. Make them, at some point, alone
with the fact of what they are.

### 4.3 The trap: the manipulative POV

**Never let the POV manipulate the reader.** If the character is charming, the prose
must not be charmed. The gap between how the character reads and how the prose
reads is the entire moral apparatus of the book.

> In *The First Law*, Glokta's narration is funny. The events are not funny. The
> prose does not wink.

## 5. Trauma: writing it without using it

Trauma is the most-abused resource in modern fantasy. The corpus's rules:

### 5.1 Rules

1. **Show the residue, not the event.** The reader needs the *consequence*, and
   mostly does not need the incident — or needs it late, and small.
2. **The event is never symmetrical.** Trauma doesn't arrive with a moral shape.
   It is not a punishment, and it's not always the character's fault. It's a thing
   that happened, and the person is left holding it.
3. **The character's coping is the characterisation.** Everyone has an adaptation.
   The interesting question is never "what happened to her" but "what did she build
   to survive it?"
4. **No cure.** Not within the book, not usually. The studied corpus is nearly
   unanimous: recovery is a *process with a cost*, not a scene where someone
   decides to be okay.
5. **Never use trauma as a plot device for someone else.** A character's trauma is
   not there to be discovered by the protagonist as leverage. (A corollary: if a
   chapter's purpose is to reveal a secret wound, be suspicious of it.)

### 5.2 The adaptation taxonomy

Useful for building believable survivors:

| Adaptation | Behaviour | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| **Hypervigilance** | Scans rooms; exits early; sleeps near a door | Exhaustion; missed intimacy |
| **Overcontrol** | Cannot let go; cannot be spontaneous | Brittle; explodes under small pressure |
| **Dissociation** | Observes from outside; reports accurately about nothing | Present but unreachable |
| **Aggression** | Pre-emptive hostility | Isolated; trusted wrongly |
| **Appeasement** | Agrees; smooths; cannot hold a boundary | Used; resented |
| **Compulsion** | Repetition; ritual; counting | Time; shame |
| **Dissociated humour** | Jokes about it, before anyone else can | Makes it unbearable for others |

### 5.3 The realism check

The single most common failure in the studied corpus's *romantasy* strand is the
**weaponised trauma** — a character's past is mentioned constantly as a shorthand
for their darkness, with no behaviour attached. A heroine whose entire psychology
is "she's broken" and who expresses this by having sad thoughts about it, is not
psychologically rendered; she's *labelled*.

**Test:** can you describe a character's psychological state using only their
**behaviours**? If you can only describe it using their history, the rendering has
failed.

## 6. The contradiction, in practice

Here is the engine at work, in a form directly usable.

**Kessa's six parts:**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Want** | To be left alone, and to be left alone *with her work*, and to never need anyone again |
| **Need** | To be depended on by someone who can see her clearly |
| **Wound** | At nineteen she left a household where she was the competent one, and came home eight months later to find she had been replaced and nobody had told her |
| **Lie** | "Needing people is how you get left." |
| **Fear** | Being useful to someone and then being unnecessary to them |
| **Contradiction** | She is fiercely loyal to people who have never asked her for loyalty, and cannot say why |

**How that contradiction appears on the page:**
- She refuses the invitation to the wedding (she is loyal to people who haven't
  asked).
- She stays three hours helping with the washing-up and doesn't explain why (she is
  the competent one; competence is the only form of belonging she trusts).
- She tells the newcomer she doesn't care whether she comes or not, in a tone that
  makes it a lie, and the lie is the first thing the reader sees.

**The contradiction makes every other element produce behaviour instead of
description.** That's the whole point.

## 7. Antagonists

The studied corpus's best antagonists are **people who have a reason**.

| Antagonist | Their reason | Their contradiction |
|---|---|---|
| A king who believes the realm will die without him | Genuine | Will not delegate, therefore cannot be corrected |
| A man who sold his family to survive | Genuine | Cannot accept that he was right |
| A general who enforces a peace that requires atrocity | Genuine | Treats his own soldiers better than the enemy |
| A god who requires worship to preserve the world | Genuine | It is not certain the world requires it |
| A thief who believes theft is honest | Genuine | Wants to be loved for something other than his skill |

**The rules:**

1. **The antagonist's reason must be stated accurately at least once**, in their own
   voice, without the narrator undercutting it.
2. **The antagonist must be right about something.** Not about everything. Something.
   If they're wrong about everything, they're a cartoon; if they're right about
   something, they're a person.
3. **The antagonist never explains their plan.** Explanation is the reader's job.
4. **The antagonist gets a POV** in the studied corpus, almost without exception, and
   the POV is where they become human. Erikson puts them at a distance; Lee puts them
   close; Abercrombie gives them the most visceral pages in the book.

## 8. Character psychology self-check

For each POV character:

1. **Want vs. need** — are they different? If they're the same, there's no arc.
2. **Wound** — can you name the specific event, in a specific place, on a specific
   day?
3. **Lie** — can you state their false belief in one sentence, in their words?
4. **Contradiction** — what two impossible things do they hold?
5. **Rendered?** — is any of this *shown* rather than *described* on the page?
6. **Residue** — does their history change what they do **this page**?
7. **Cost** — what did their last choice take from them?
8. **Predictability** — would the reader have predicted their choice? If yes, they're
   a function, not a person.
9. **The gap** — do they hold a false model of someone the reader can see clearly?
10. **Competence** — are they good at something specific, on the page?
