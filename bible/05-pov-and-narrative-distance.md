# 05 · Point of View & Narrative Distance

**POV as a lens. Information control as the engine of serial fiction.**

---

## 1. The thesis

POV is not a camera position. It is a **filter on attention**, and it has three
independent dials:

| Dial | What it controls |
|---|---|
| **Who** | Whose consciousness filters the prose |
| **How close** | How much of their interiority we get (close / medium / distant) |
| **How much they know** | How wrong they can be |

Get all three right and the reader forgets they're being told a story and starts
believing they're *somewhere*.

---

## 2. The dials in practice

### 2.1 Who

The corpus overwhelmingly uses **close third limited** with a **rotating cast**.
Abercrombie, Martin, Gwynne, Lee, Sullivan, Hobb (in her non-Farseer books),
Johansen — all rotate. Only *Outlander* and Hobb's *Farseer* books hold a single
voice for the whole book, and both use that to make the voice itself the point.

**House default:** 4–8 POV characters, rotated so that no character appears twice
in a row without good reason, and so that no two consecutive chapters are from
characters on the same side of a conflict.

### 2.2 How close

| Distance | Effect | Use when |
|---|---|---|
| **Intimate** (free indirect discourse, deep interiority) | The reader *is* the character. Maximum empathy, minimum objectivity. | The protagonist's chapters. The emotional core. |
| **Middle** (thoughts present but not continuous) | The reader accompanies. | Supporting POVs. |
| **Distant / external** (thoughts absent or reported) | The reader watches a person they can read. Effective for *mystery*, *dramatic irony*, and characters whose interiority is a secret. | Antagonists, characters who won't be themselves, horror POV. |

**The underused move:** an antagonist in distant POV. The reader knows what the
antagonist wants and can't say anything. The tension becomes inevitable and
unavoidable.

### 2.3 How much they know

This is the dial most writers forget. **A POV character does not know what the
reader knows.**

The corpus does this relentlessly. Jordan's characters think they know what's
happening and are routinely, expensively wrong; other characters *see* them being
wrong and the reader gets both. Abercrombie's Glokta is systematically
misinterpreting the people around him, and the comedy and the tragedy both come
from the gap between his model and reality.

## 3. The rules

### Rule 1 — No head-hopping mid-scene
A POV chapter is one consciousness. If you want to see another character's interiority,
break the chapter.

**Exception:** the mid-scene switch, used sparingly and well. *Jade City* switches
POV mid-chapter; *Malazan* switches POV mid-scene without a break; the studied
corpus uses this to produce a specific effect — a reveal landing harder because the
reader is moved to a different witness. If you do it, do it *once* per book and make
it earn its cost.

### Rule 2 — The narrator cannot know more than the POV
The single most common first-draft error.

> ❌ "She didn't know it yet, but he had already decided to leave."
> ✅ "He had decided to leave. He was careful not to look at her while he decided."

The first is an authorial aside and it kills the POV for the rest of the paragraph.
The second keeps us inside her ignorance while telling us what we need to know.

### Rule 3 — Everything the POV perceives is filtered
Not just the thinking. The **selection** is filtered. This is Abercrombie's central
technique and it is the highest-value craft lesson in the study:

> A farrier looks at a courtyard and sees the horses' hooves, the state of the
> gelding's teeth, whether a stranger's boots have been resoled recently. A
> bureaucrat sees who is standing near whom. A grieving person notices nothing at
> all and is embarrassed about it.

**House test:** read a paragraph and ask: *what would this person notice that nobody
else would?* If the answer is "nothing specific," the paragraph is camera footage.

### Rule 4 — Unreliable narration is bias, not lying
The best unreliable narrators are **honest and wrong**, not deceptive. They report
what they saw, accurately, and misinterpret it.

| Mechanism | Effect | Example use |
|---|---|---|
| **Sincere error** | Reader knows more than the POV | The whole of *The Poppy War*'s first half |
| **Partial attention** | The POV doesn't notice the important thing | Assassination scenes, overheard conversations |
| **Self-justification** | POV explains away evidence | Tyrion, Logen, Glokta |
| **Retrospective distance** | Older narrator, memory reorganised | Hobb's *Farseer* — the further back the event, the less reliable the telling |
| **Ideological frame** | POV reads everything through a lens | A soldier reading a peace treaty; a zealot reading kindness |

**The retrospective mechanism deserves special mention.** Robin Hobb's *Farseer*
books are narrated by an older Fitz remembering a younger Fitz — and because the
older Fitz has been through things that the younger one hasn't, **his memory of
early events is systematically less reliable than his memory of recent ones.** The
reader learns, late, that some of what they were told was wrong. This is the most
elegant unreliability in the corpus, and it costs nothing at the sentence level.

### Rule 5 — Never let the POV be the story's moral arbiter
The reader decides. If a POV's judgement is right and the prose endorses it, you
have written propaganda.

This is why the strongest corpus works have POVs we don't fully trust, and why the
weakest (in the studied set) tend toward protagonists who are reliably right about
everyone else.

## 4. Information architecture in serial fiction

The core mechanic of popular serialised fantasy is **distributed information**.

```
        POV A knows X, doesn't know Y
        POV B knows Y, doesn't know X
        POV C knows both but believes something wrong
        The READER knows X and Y and the wrong belief
```

The reader's position — knowing more than any character, and knowing that at least
one character is wrong — is the engine of the whole form.

**Consequences:**

1. **No character can explain the plot.** The narrative voice is not available to
   summarise. If a POV were allowed to know everything, the reader's advantage
   evaporates.
2. **Foreshadowing is structural, not decorative.** A detail planted in POV A's
   chapter is a fact the reader holds when POV B contradicts it. That's the payoff.
3. **The turn works better.** Because the reader has been assembling a picture,
   a reversal doesn't just surprise — it *re-contextualises*, which is far more
   satisfying than new information alone.
4. **Suspense is cheap and reliable.** Something is going to happen that some
   character doesn't know is coming. The reader waits for the collision.

> *Malazan*'s structure, in which the same events are later told from multiple
> viewpoints so that the reader understands "nothing in this world is necessarily
> true simply because we read it," is the extreme version of this principle. It's
> a difficult model to sustain and it's the most powerful demonstration in the
> corpus that **the reader's position in the information is the real protagonist
> of serialised fiction.**

## 5. Managing a large cast

The corpus with the biggest cast — *Malazan*, ~3.3 million words — solves it with
**modular grouping**: characters cluster into units (a company of soldiers, a
ship's crew, a family), each unit gets its own internal POV, and the reader tracks
the unit rather than the individual.

The cast with the most surgical solution — *Jade City* — uses **institutional
identity**: characters are defined by their role in a hierarchy (Pillar, Horn,
Fingers, Luckbringers, Students), so the reader learns the structure once and reads
the roles as a grammar.

**House method — the four questions for every POV:**

1. **What does this character want, right now, in this scene?**
2. **What do they think is the obstacle?** (Note: not the real obstacle.)
3. **What do they *not* know that the reader knows?**
4. **What would they do that a reader would not predict?**

If you can't answer 3 and 4, that character doesn't need a POV chapter. They can be
a scene, a name, or a rumour.

## 6. First person: when and how

First person is used in the corpus for three distinct effects:

| Effect | Example |
|---|---|
| **Intimacy maximum** | *Outlander* — Claire's wry, precise, modern observer filtering an 18th-century world |
| **Retrospective unreliability** | *Farseer* — older Fitz's reorganised memory |
| **Voice as characterisation** | *The Second Apocalypse* — the narrator as a witness with a perspective |

**First person costs:** you must filter the prose through a *voice*, not just a
*mind*. A first-person narrator has an opinion about everything, and the opinion is
part of the information.

**The first-person trap:** the narrator ends up describing rather than experiencing.
> ❌ "He was a tall man with grey hair who wore a sword."
> ✅ "He was the tallest man I'd met that year, and I'd met a lot of them, and he
> wore his sword the way my father wore his cane — like a thing he'd stopped
> needing to think about."

**House rule:** in first person, at least one of the following must be true of the
narrator — they're funny, they're wrong, they're hiding something, or they're
watching something they wish they weren't.

---

## 7. The dropped-POV problem and how to solve it

When a POV stops being interesting — because their arc has concluded, or because
their situation has become repetitive — the corpus's answer is not to keep writing
them. It's to **change their function**:

- Promote them to a different register (closer, or more distant).
- Change what they *want* without changing who they are.
- Let them become a **witness for someone else's chapter** — appear in the scene
  without holding the POV.
- Give them a **cost** they couldn't previously afford.
- Retire them honourably with an ending scene that closes their arc.

**Do not** keep writing a POV because the outline says they'll be in chapter 40.

---

## 8. POV self-check

1. **Filter** — what would *this* character notice that nobody else would?
2. **Knowledge** — any sentence where the narrator knows more than the POV?
3. **Selection** — is the POV noticing anything at all that isn't plot-relevant?
4. **Belief** — is the POV's model of the world doing any work, or is the prose
   just reporting?
5. **Gap** — is there something the reader knows that this character doesn't?
6. **Predictability** — would you have predicted their choice? If yes, they're
   passive.
7. **Rotation** — no two consecutive chapters from the same character? No two
   consecutive chapters from the same side?
