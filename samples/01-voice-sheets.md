# Samples · Voice Sheets

**Six worked specimens. The template for building a character whose voice you can
recognise in the first paragraph.**

---

## The template

```
NAME:
SIX-PART ENGINE
  WANT:      (conscious, specific, not the plot's goal for them)
  NEED:      (unconscious)
  WOUND:     (event / place / year)
  LIE:       (false belief, in their words)
  FEAR:      (specific dread)
  CONTRADICTION: (two impossible things they hold)
VOICE
  Vocabulary:         Contractions:      Sentence length:
  Humour:             Formality:         Evasion:
  Punctuation habit:                  TABOO SUBJECT:
FILTER
  What they notice that others don't:
  Perceptual limitation:
  What they misread about people:
COMPETENCE (shown on the page):
THE GAP (false model they hold of someone):
```

## 1. Malle Osk — the drowned archivist

```
WANT:      To finish the index before the water takes the lower stacks.
NEED:      To be a person who is loved, not a person who is useful.
WOUND:     Winter, in a town with a name like a knot, when the archive she was
           indexing caught and she ran for the door and left four people in it.
LIE:       "If I'm needed, I'm safe."
FEAR:      Being thanked. She cannot answer it and she has never found out why.
CONTRADICTION: She would die for the work, and she has twice chosen a person over
           the work, and she has never once told the work that this happened.
```

**Voice**
- Vocabulary: plain, exact, archival. She names things by their function.
- Contractions: none. Not affectation — she was taught.
- Sentence length: medium, with an occasional long one when she is listing.
- Humour: dead, and only about herself, and only when she is frightened.
- Formality: high with strangers, drops entirely with two people.
- Evasion: answers a nearby question. Never the question asked.
- Punctuation habit: semicolons. Long parenthetical asides.
- **Taboo subject: her sister, who was in the archive.**

**Filter**
- Notices: damp. Always damp. And the *order* of things — she catalogues
  instinctively, and the reader can see her doing it.
- Perceptual limitation: she cannot stop cataloguing. She names the objects in a
  room before she sees the person in it.
- What she misreads about people: she reads competence as love. She thinks people
  want her because she's useful, and is baffled when they don't.

**Competence:** she can find anything, in any system, that has been catalogued —
including systems nobody has catalogued.

**The Gap:** she believes her sister didn't try to reach her.

**In the prose:**
> Malle put the two bowls down, the wooden one on the left and the glazed one on
> the right, and adjusted the smaller by a half-inch so that the rims were
> parallel, and only then looked up. There was a woman in the doorway with rain on
> the hem of her coat, and Malle registered the coat, and the wet, and the fact that
> the coat was good, and the fact that the good coat had come out in this weather,
> and then she registered the face, and the face was the one she'd been not
> thinking about for eleven years.

## 2. Sergeant Cael Ordry — the professional witness

```
WANT:      To get through the winter with his section and a full report.
NEED:      To be told he was wrong, once, by someone whose opinion he trusts.
WOUND:     A decision he made at a ford, in a language he didn't speak, on a
           night in the year the fever came. He has never learned what he said.
LIE:       "The report is the truth."
FEAR:      Silence in the people who follow him.
CONTRADICTION: He has never once made a decision that was not written down, and
           the one that mattered most was not.
```

**Voice**
- Vocabulary: plain, procedural, slightly old-fashioned. Uses the words of the
  manual — *strength, march, halt, formed*.
- Contractions: minimal. Formal by trade.
- Sentence length: **short.** He is a man who gives orders.
- Humour: rare, and it comes out sideways — a correction delivered as pedantry.
- Formality: ranks are addressed by rank. Always.
- Evasion: he will not explain a decision. He will repeat it.
- Punctuation habit: full stops. No dashes. No ellipses.
- **Taboo subject: the ford.**

**Filter**
- Notices: numbers. Strengths. Who is not where they should be.
- Perceptual limitation: he counts. He counts everything, and he can't stop, and
  the counting is a substitute for deciding.
- What she misreads about people: he reads hesitation as hostility, because
  hesitation in his experience preceded ambush.

**Competence:** he can take a section of forty across broken ground in the dark
and get them there with the numbers unchanged.

**The Gap:** he believes the report of the ford was accurate because he wrote it
himself.

**In the prose:**
> Ordry counted them again, because the first count had come out thirty-eight and
> it had been thirty-nine at the ford, and there was no arithmetic that got from
> thirty-nine to thirty-eight without a man having walked off in the dark on his
> own recognizance, and men did not do that, not from him, not in his country.
>
> "Corporal."
>
> "Sir."
>
> "Count again."
>
> The corporal counted again. It took a while, because the corporal was thorough,
> which was why Ordry had wanted him.
>
> "Thirty-eight, Sergeant."
>
> "Thirty-eight," Ordry agreed, and did not say anything else, and the fire sank
> another inch while he did not say anything else.

## 3. Ash Vell — the one who sells

```
WANT:      To be somewhere else by spring, with enough money to not go back.
NEED:      To be known. Actually known, by one person, without the transaction.
WOUND:     Sold, at nine, to a man who was decent about it, which was worse.
LIE:       "Everything has a price and I know what things are worth."
FEAR:      Being given something for nothing.
CONTRADICTION: She is the most honest person in the room and she has never in her
           life given anyone the true figure.
```

**Voice**
- Vocabulary: transactional, specific, funny. Uses prices and units.
- Contractions: heavy.
- Sentence length: medium; short when she's closing.
- Humour: constant, and it is *real* — she finds things funny. She's not bitter,
  she's busy.
- Formality: matches whoever she's talking to, instantly and exactly.
- Evasion: she gives a price instead of an answer.
- Punctuation habit: em-dashes, used precisely; she'll stop mid-sentence when she's
  satisfied.
- **Taboo subject: the man who bought her. She will discuss any transaction but
  that one.**

**Filter**
- Notices: value. What things cost, what's worn out, what's about to break.
- Perceptual limitation: she evaluates. Everything, constantly, including
  people.
- What she misreads about people: she assumes everyone wants something she can
  name.

**Competence:** she can price anything, including a person's loyalty, and she can
usually get close.

**The Gap:** she believes the woman who bought her back in the early spring was
her mother.

**In the prose:**
> "Two hundred," Ash said, "and I'll take the horse."
>
> "The horse is worth four."
>
> "The horse is worth four and it's got a bad hoof and a bad temperament and it's
> been up the pass in weather, and you'd be buying four horses and a problem."
>
> "That's still more than two hundred."
>
> "It's less than two-fifty, though, if you want to call it a problem and not an
> accident." She held out her hand, palm up, and waited, because she had learned
> that people who were about to argue about money would give you the money first
> and then argue about it afterwards, out of politeness, which was a kind of
> violence she had never had the patience to understand.

## 4. The Widow Arren — the one who has already paid

```
WANT:      To see her son buried in the field her husband chose.
NEED:      To be told it was worth it.
WOUND:     Not one event. Four years, in a house with a bad roof, with a boy
           who was ill, and a decision made in the third year.
LIE:       "I did what was available."
FEAR:      Relief. She has been afraid of feeling relieved for two years.
CONTRADICTION: She has never forgiven anyone and she has never refused anyone.
```

**Voice**
- Vocabulary: domestic, precise, warm. She knows plants, cloth, weather, food.
- Contractions: moderate.
- Sentence length: **long**, run-on, gathering clauses — the way people speak when
  they have been talking to themselves for two years.
- Humour: warm, self-directed, and always slightly ahead of itself.
- Formality: none; she calls everyone by a domestic title.
- Evasion: she changes the subject to something physical — the bread, the weather.
- Punctuation habit: commas where other people use full stops; her sentences run on
  because she is thinking while she talks.
- **Taboo subject: the third year.**

**Filter**
- Notices: food. Weather. The state of things — roofs, sleeves, wood.
- Perceptual limitation: she can't stop assessing whether people have eaten.
- What she misreads about people: she assumes everyone is hiding something because
  she is always hiding something.

**Competence:** she can stretch a bad winter into a survivable one. She has done it
twice.

**The Gap:** she believes her son doesn't remember the third year.

**In the prose:**
> "You'll want to come in, you'll be frozen, and I've put the bread on and there's
> more of it than there's likely to be, which I don't say to be kind, I say because
> it's true and I'd rather you knew the state of things than found out at
> supper — sit, sit, the stool's sound, the other one went last winter — and don't
> say anything about the boy, I know, I know, I only meant that the bed in the
> back room is made up and it's not as good as the front but it's dry and there's
> a lamp, and I'm not saying it for him, I'm saying it because I'm a woman who
> thinks about the damp." She was still talking when she put the kettle on, and
> she did not turn round.

## 5. Iven Mark — the one who is right

```
WANT:      To finish the survey and be believed about the boundary.
NEED:      To be wrong, publicly, once, in a way that costs him.
WOUND:     He was right about the boundary as a boy and the men who did not
           believe him died, and he has decided that being right is a thing that
           happens to other people.
LIE:       "I only say what the evidence says."
FEAR:      Silence after he's spoken.
CONTRADICTION: He is honest about facts and completely dishonest about his own
           motives, and he has never told anyone which of his statements is
           about himself.
```

**Voice**
- Vocabulary: technical, Latinate, precise — the register of power (`10`).
- Contractions: none.
- Sentence length: **long**, with subordinate clauses, and the main clause always
  at the *end*.
- Humour: none. When he's funny it's an accident and he doesn't notice.
- Formality: extreme. He ends sentences with periods even when addressing a
  superior informally.
- Evasion: he answers the question *behind* the question.
- Punctuation habit: semicolons and parentheses; he qualifies everything.
- **Taboo subject: the boundary.**

**Filter**
- Notices: discrepancies. Anything that doesn't match. He reads the world as a
  series of claims and counter-claims.
- Perceptual limitation: he can't stop looking for the discrepancy, including in
  people, and this makes him misread kindness as evasion.
- What she misreads about people: he assumes everyone is withholding something
  because he always is.

**Competence:** he can reconstruct a boundary from a map and three oral accounts
and he is never wrong about where a line is.

**The Gap:** he believes his father was wrong, and reads every document of his
father's as an attempt to mislead.

**In the prose:**
> "The eastern boundary cannot be what the survey describes, and I would suggest,
> if it is permissible to raise the point at this stage, that the survey has
> described — with respect — the intention rather than the practice, which is a
  distinction that the earlier sheets have not been careful to make, and which the
> Treasury, in my experience, has not been in the habit of noticing, since the
> intention is on the record and the practice is, of course, only on the ground."
>
> Nobody said anything for a moment.
>
> "That is a considerable charge," said the assessor.
>
> "It is a small observation," said Iven Mark. "The ground is where I have been."

## 6. Talia Renn — the one who is learning

```
WANT:      To be told she is good at this.
NEED:      To be good at this for reasons she chose, not because she was assigned.
WOUND:     A year in a house where she was the one who was cleaned up after, and
           a woman who said, kindly, that she had a gift for it.
LIE:       "Being needed is the same as being wanted."
FEAR:      The moment after she finishes helping and nobody has said anything.
CONTRADICTION: She has decided to be an assassin and she is constitutionally
           unable to leave a person in distress.
```

**Voice**
- Vocabulary: plain but *new* — she's reading widely and it shows, and she uses
  the wrong word in the wrong place occasionally, and it gets corrected.
- Contractions: moderate, and she over-uses "I mean."
- Sentence length: variable; she's still finding it.
- Humour: quick, nervous, mostly at herself.
- Formality: low; she's still learning the registers.
- Evasion: she talks past the question by being charming.
- Punctuation habit: dashes; she starts sentences and abandons them.
- **Taboo subject: the woman who said she had a gift.**

**Filter**
- Notices: exits, windows, who is watching, and the emotional state of the room.
- Perceptual limitation: she catalogues threats before she engages with people,
  and it makes her seem cold in the first half hour of every scene.
- What she misreads about people: she assumes affection is a transaction because
  the only affection she was taught was transactional.

**Competence:** she is genuinely excellent with people. She can get a stranger to
tell her the truth in four minutes.

**The Gap:** she believes the woman who trained her was acting out of affection.

**In the prose:**
> "You're the new one."
>
> "Talia Renn. Six weeks." She stopped, and then, because she had learned in six
> weeks that stopping was worse than the sentence: "Talia Renn. Six weeks. I'm — I
> mean, I'm good at the talking part, I think. Someone said. I haven't—" She
> started again. "Sorry."
>
> "Hennan said that."
>
> "Did he."
>
> "He says it about everyone. It's not a compliment, it's a job description." The
> woman looked at her for slightly too long, in the way people did when they were
> deciding how much to say, and Talia felt the doors and the windows come up in
> her, unbidden, the way they always did, and had a moment of thinking that this
> was going to be a problem she was going to have to fix later and probably would
> not.

## The exercise

Take your own cast. Fill in the template for each POV character, then:

1. **Write 150 words of narration from each**, on the same scene. No dialogue.
2. **Read them aloud, in sequence, without the headings.** If you can tell them
   apart, the voice sheets are working.
3. If you can't, the differences are in the *content* of the sheet rather than the
   *voice* section. Go back and complete the voice section properly — that's
   where the work is.
4. **Then** check each one's filter. What do they notice that nobody else would?
   If the answer is "nothing", the character has no interior life and the chapter
   will feel like a transcript.

---

## The two-voice test

The most common failure in serial fantasy is that all the POVs sound like the same
author. Here is the fastest test:

> Put two POV chapters side by side, delete the character names, and give them to
> someone who hasn't read the book. Ask them to attribute each to a character.
>
> If they can't, you have one voice and several heads. Go back to the voice sheets
> and find the differences in **vocabulary, sentence length, and what they notice**.
> Those three are enough to make a character legible on the page.
