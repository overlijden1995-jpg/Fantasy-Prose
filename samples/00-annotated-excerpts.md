# Samples · Annotated Excerpts

**Original prose written for this repository, demonstrating each law.**

Every passage here was written for the Fantasy-Prose project. Nothing is quoted from
the studied works. Each excerpt is followed by the law it demonstrates and the
diagnostic that catches its failure.

---

## 1. Law I — Specificity is the whole job

### The failure (rung 2: categorical adjective)

> She moved through the room and everything about her was beautiful and everyone
> noticed and the room went quiet.

### The house (rung 5: material)

> She came in through the servant door with her hood still up, and the room went
> quiet in the way a room does when it has decided to be careful. She was not
> beautiful. That had been arranged by her mother and it was a family resemblance
> and she had never needed it before tonight and she found, standing in the doorway
> with the mud drying on her boots, that she could not remember the last time
> anyone had looked at her and seen anything but the arrangement.

**What's happening:** no emotion has been named. The reader has been given (1) a
door that says something about her status, (2) mud on boots, (3) a family's
arrangement of her face, (4) a physical fact about her relationship with her own
appearance, (5) the room's reaction, rendered as a *social* fact rather than an
emotion.

**Diagnostic:** can you swap the muddy boots for a different detail and keep the
scene intact? If yes, they were decoration.

## 2. Law II — Every character is a lens

The same courtyard, three POVs. Only the *attention* changes.

### A farrier

> The stranger's boots had been resoled twice and the second time had been done
> badly, with too little hob, and there was a place on the left heel where the
> stitching had gone. They weren't a soldier's boots. A soldier's would have been
> resoled by the regimental cobbler, who did it well, or not at all.

### A bureaucrat

> The stranger stood to the left of the pillar, which was where people stood when
> they wanted to be seen waiting and not seen waiting, and he had arrived before the
> third bell, which meant he had come to the north gate and not the south, which
> meant he had a reason to avoid the Custom House.

### A woman whose son has gone missing

> The stranger was wearing good boots. He had come in from the wet and he had not
> troubled to change them, and there was chalk on the toe of the left one, the
> kind that came off a wall, and there was a wall in this city with chalk on it
> that had a list on it, and it was four streets over, and the stranger had come
> from the direction of it and not the direction of anywhere else.

**What's happening:** the world is identical. The reader's understanding of the
*scene* is identical too. What changes is what the reader knows about the person.
And in the third version, the reader has learned something enormous — a lead, a
direction, a wall with a list on it — without a word of exposition and without the
character knowing what it means.

**Diagnostic:** swap two paragraphs between POVs. If nothing breaks, the POVs aren't
filters yet.

## 3. Law III — The narration knows less than the writer

### The failure

> She didn't know it yet, but he had already made his decision, and it would cost
> her the house.

### The house

> He'd made his decision on the road, in the rain, with the horse's shoulder
> knocking his knee, and he had not moved since, and she could see that he had not
> moved, and she decided not to ask, and the not-asking took the whole rest of the
> afternoon.

**What's happening:** the reader knows what the POV knows (that a decision has been
made) and what she suspects (that it concerns her). The *content* of the decision
is withheld — but it isn't withheld from the reader by authorial intrusion; it's
withheld because it hasn't happened in the scene yet. Nothing in the narration
reaches outside her head.

**Diagnostic:** highlight every sentence where the narrator knows more than the
POV. Cut or relocate.

## 4. Law IV — Dialogue is the instrument

### A bargain (every line does two or three jobs)

> "I want the ledger."
>
> "You want the ledger."
>
> "I want the ledger, and I want you to stop asking me why."
>
> "That's two wants."
>
> "It's one want and a condition."
>
> He poured the second cup and did not drink it. "You know what the second cup's
> for."
>
> "I know what the second cup's for. I want the ledger."
>
> "Then drink it."
>
> "Then you'll give me the ledger."
>
> "Then you'll drink it." He turned the cup a quarter-turn on the table, which was
> the only concession he had ever made to anything, and she looked at it, and then
> at him, and then she drank it, and it was not good, and she drank all of it.

**Job audit:**
- "I want the ledger" — advance + characterise (directness) + texture (the economy
  of the relationship).
- "That's two wants." — pressure + subtext (he is teaching her how to negotiate).
- "It's one want and a condition." — characterise (she's better at this than he is)
  + subtext (she's teaching him).
- The quarter-turn — texture (a habit that *is* his psychology) + advance (it's the
  concession).
- The last three lines — advance (she's paid) + texture (the drink is bad) +
  the turn (he got what he wanted and it cost her).

**Diagnostic:** remove all the tags. Could anyone say these lines? The whole
sequence collapses without its specificity of *who says what*.

## 5. Law V — Violence has a ledger

### Mechanism

> He put the blade in under the sternum and leaned on it, because the coat had
> turned it and leaning was what there was instead of cutting. The man made a
> sound like a man who had been about to say something. Then he was on the boards
> and the boards were wet and getting wetter.

### Consequence, in a different scene, forty pages later

> She could not lift the left hand above the shoulder. It had not been that way
> since the woodshed, and she had thought it would be by now, and she had not
> asked, because asking would have meant remembering the woodshed on purpose
> instead of by accident, and she had been keeping it by accident for four months.

### Aftermath, in its own scene

> They washed the boards twice and then the third time with the bucket, and the
> water went grey and then it went black and then there was nothing left in it to
> make it anything, and his sister came in at the end and looked at the floor and
> did not ask and started on the other side of the room with the second bucket.
> Neither of them said anything for a while. It was the first silence between them
> in eleven months and it was, she would think later, the worst thing about the
> whole year, and it was also the last time she was ever able to think about the
> woodshed without her brother standing in the doorway with the bucket.

**What's happening:** the violence happened in one scene, the consequence in
another, and the *aftermath* in a third, forty pages later, in a scene about
cleaning. Nobody reflects on the killing in the paragraph after the killing. The
reflection arrives as a *domestic arrangement* — a silence between siblings, and a
doorway. The grief is rendered as behaviour and as a physical fact (the hand that
cannot lift), and the hand is the ledger.

**Diagnostic:** for every wound, find the later page. If there isn't one, it's set
dressing.

## 6. Law VI — Consequence over complication

### The failure (wish fulfilment)

> He fought the three men and won, and though he was cut across the ribs he was
> too brave to stop, and the crowd cheered, and he was proud.

### The house

> He won. That was the whole of it, and it took less time than the woman had
> predicted, and when it was finished he stood in the yard with the rain going
> through the front of his shirt and the blood coming out of the ribs in a way
> that was slower than he had expected it to be, and he thought, with something
> close to surprise, that he had not known that was available to him: that he
> could still do it, and that the cost of it would arrive afterwards and be
> somebody else's business.
>
> The surgeon came at the second hour and did what he could, and it was enough
> for the woman to sit up, which was what she needed him for, and not enough for
> him to be there on the fourth day when she needed him to be there on the fourth
> day. He was not there on the fourth day. He had a reason, and the reason was
> good, and he never entirely believed it.

**What's happening:** the victory is real and the cost arrives *later*, in a
different currency. The prose doesn't moralise. It just tells you about the
fourth day and the reason and the belief, and the reader draws the conclusion.

**Diagnostic:** did anyone pay? If every character left the scene whole, you
wrote a wish fulfilment.

## 7. Law VII — The world is a set of pressures

### The failure (info-dump)

> The Salt Compact had been agreed in the year 311 between the seven river lords,
> and it bound the wardens of every crossing on the March to read the water before
> any crossing was permitted, and it was the reason the March had no war for two
> hundred years, and the lords who had signed it were long dead, and nobody
> remembered their names.

### The house

> "You'll not cross at Lind," she said. "The salt's in the water there and the
> wardens read it."
>
> "They read salt."
>
> "They read what's in it. Bodies. Boats. Whether the water's been salted." She
> spat into the ditch. "You want to cross at Lind, cross at the stone bridge and
> take the long road, and if you're carrying anything the March doesn't want
> carried, you'll want to take it over the ford at Ashlett instead, and don't ask
> me why."
>
> "Why not?"
>
> "Because you'll tell me a reason and I'll have to remember it, and I've a long
> way to walk." She was already walking. "And because it's not mine to sell."

**What the reader now has, in nine lines:** a March; wardens; salt; reading;
bodies and boats in the water; a stone bridge; a long road; a ford at Ashlett; a
man carrying something unwanted; a woman who won't say why; and a relationship
that is closing. Zero exposition. Everything is pressure.

**Diagnostic:** who wanted this information? What did they get for it?

## 8. Law VIII — Memory is a character with a grudge

### The involuntary cue

> The woman turned and put her hand on the latch of the door, and stopped, and her
> other hand went to the place on her wrist where the scar was, without her
> seeming to have decided to do it, and she looked at the door as though the door
> had done something.
>
> "Is there anyone else," she said. "In the yard."
>
> "No."
>
> "Then I'll go down."
>
> She did not go down. She stood with her hand on the latch for the length of a
> breath and then she went out, and the yard was empty, and it had been empty all
> along.

**What's happening:** we never learn what happened to her. We learn that there is
a scar, that it is on her wrist, that her body goes to it before her mind
decides, and that she asked a question she already knew the answer to — and got it,
and did not believe it, and went down anyway.

**Diagnostic:** does the character's past change what they do on *this* page? If
not, it's a prop.

## 9. Law IX — The turn, not the summary

### The failure

> And so she left the palace at last, uncertain of what the future held, and slept
> fitfully that night, wondering whether she had made the right choice.

### The house

> She went down the stairs.

**Note:** the turn is also a *decision*, and the sentence is one verb. Everything
the reader needs to feel is carried by what came before and by what the decision
costs.

### A false turn (the chapter ends on something that seems to be the turn)

> **Chapter ends:** "So the debt was settled."
>
> **Next chapter, three pages in:** the man who had said so had been dead for a
> year, and the settlement had been made with his estate, and his estate had been
> paying out on debts it had never heard of, which meant that somewhere there was
> a ledger with a name in it, and the name was going to be a problem for somebody.

**Diagnostic:** does the last sentence *change* anything? If it only recaps, rewrite
the last two paragraphs.

## 10. Law X — Earn the ending of the sentence

### The failure

> She walked away into the darkness, and behind her the hall fell silent, and
> somewhere in the distance a bell began to toll, marking the hour and the end of
> that fateful day.

### The house

> She walked. Behind her the hall went quiet, and then somebody started laughing
> about the price of the horse, and she kept walking.

**What's happening:** the prose closes on a small, flat, human detail. The bell
would have told the reader something; the laughter tells the reader *the world
continues and she is not at the centre of it*. That's a different, better ending,
and it satisfies.

## 11. Romantasy mode — tension, consent, and the cost

### The misread (the "Grudging Turn")

> "I thought you might—" He stopped and looked at the fire.
>
> "Might what."
>
> "Nothing."
>
> "You don't get to do that."
>
> "I do when it's about me." He turned the cup on the table, the way he did, the
> quarter-turn, which she had learned to read as a thing he did when he was
> deciding whether to tell the truth and had decided to tell a different one.
> "You asked me once if I had ever been in love."
>
> "I didn't."
>
> "You did. You asked whether it was possible. Whether you'd been capable of it."
> He still wasn't looking at her. "I said I didn't know. That was a lie. I knew. I
> have known for a very long time, and I have been telling myself it was a
> technical question, and it was not a technical question, and you are going to
> ask me who, and I am going to tell you, and after that nothing is going to be
> the same, and I would like you to sit down first."
>
> "You could have said."
>
> "I could have said."
>
> "When."
>
> "Every day for three years," he said, "and every day I did not, and that is the
> answer and there is no better one."

**What's happening:**
- The turn is a **reveal**, prepared by the quarter-turn (a habit introduced
  earlier in the book, so the reader can predict it *and* dread it).
- The Grudging Turn is not "he's nicer than I thought." It's "the friction was a
  misreading" — she'd asked a question she thought was hypothetical.
- The consent beat: he asks her to sit down. He doesn't touch her. He doesn't
  assert. He gives her the *information* and then asks for her to be in a condition
  to receive it. **Refusal is safe**: she can stand up and leave, and the book
  doesn't punish it.
- The cost lands in the past tense of his own behaviour: three years of daily
  choice not to say it.

**Diagnostic:** would the reader have predicted this reveal? If yes, the preparation
worked. If they predicted *this specific shape* of reveal, plant something else.

## 12. The register ladder — the same scene at three temperatures

The scene: two people, a table, a document.

### 0 — Cold realism

> He put the paper down. "No."
>
> "You haven't read it."
>
> "I don't need to." He turned it back toward her. "Your father's signature is
> wrong. It's a good forgery and it's the wrong hand."
>
> She looked at it for a while. "Who taught you?"
>
> "Somebody you wouldn't have liked."

### 4 — Lush & dark

> He laid the paper down on the table between them, and the candlelight found the
> wetness of the ink and made a small bright country of it on the page.
>
> "No."
>
> "You haven't read it."
>
> "I don't need to." He turned it back toward her, and the wax of the seal
> complained against the wood as it went. "Your father's hand is wrong. It is a
> lovely forgery, and it is the wrong hand, and the wrong hand is a dead man's."
>
> She did not touch it. She looked at the signature for a long moment, and the
> long moment had a shape to it, and the shape was the shape of a woman standing
> at a window in a house she had not been in for nine years.
>
> "Who taught you?"
>
> "Somebody you wouldn't have liked."

### 8 — Poetic / mythic

> He set it down between them, the page and the pool of it, the wet ink still
> shining, a small lit country in the middle of the table.
>
> "No."
>
> "You have not read it."
>
> "I have need of no reading." He turned it toward her again, and the seal
> complained against the wood. "Your father's hand is wrong. It is a good forgery.
> It is the hand of a man who has been in the ground eleven years."
>
> She did not reach for it. She looked a long while at the shape of the name, and
> the long while had a shape of its own, and the shape was the shape of a woman at
> a window in a house she had not stood in for nine years.
>
> "Who taught you this?"
>
> "Somebody you would not have liked."

**What changed between 0 and 8:** the *content* is identical. The information,
the conflict, the reveal, the turn, the subtext — all the same. What changed is the
**texture**: sentence length, sensory weight, and the density of metaphor.

**This is the point of the dial.** The dial changes the *temperature*, never the
*architecture*. If the plot of the 8 version is different from the 0 version, the
dial is being misused — you're writing a different book, not a hotter one.

## 13. The chapter turn — four kinds, side by side

**Reversal**
> The man she'd pulled out of the river had been the one who'd sold them.

**Reveal**
> The second shipment had never been a shipment.

**Decision**
> She went down the stairs.

**Arrival**
> The door opened, and it was her brother, and he was alone.

**Never:**
> And so she left, uncertain of what the future held, wondering whether she had
> made the right choice.

## 14. Filters, before and after

| Filter | Cleaned |
|---|---|
| "She saw that the door was open." | "The door was open." |
| "He noticed the smell of smoke." | "There was smoke." |
| "She realised that he was lying." | "He was lying." |
| "She felt a pang of grief." | "She put the bowl down very carefully, and then washed it, and then washed it again." |
| "He was suddenly aware of the cold." | "The cold was in his hands now." |
| "She watched him leave." | "He left." |
| "It was not anger. It was something older." | "It was something older." |
| "She was sure that the man was not what he seemed." | "She was sure of it, the way you are sure of a step that isn't there." |
